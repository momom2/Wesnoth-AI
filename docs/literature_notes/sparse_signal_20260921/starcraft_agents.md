## Key question 1: What exact mechanisms turned the win/loss bit into dense training signal, and what did the ablations show?

### Takeaway
No top SC2 agent replaced win/loss as the primary RL reward; every one of them (AlphaStar, TStarBot-X, SCC, DI-star) kept the undiscounted terminal outcome and added human-derived auxiliary signal: initialisation from imitation, a KL penalty toward the imitation policy, a sampled "strategy statistic" z with pseudo-rewards for following it, a value function that sees the opponent's state during training, and off-policy corrections (V-trace/TD(lambda)/UPGO). AlphaStar's own ablation puts numbers on each: from Elo 149 (RL from scratch) to 936 (supervised only) to 1020 (supervised init) to 1400 (+KL to supervised) to 1540 (+z statistics); the value function seeing the opponent moved best-response win rate from 22% to 82%; V-trace to +TD(lambda) to +UPGO went 49% to 73% to 82%. The cheaper agents that matched or approached AlphaStar (TStarBot-X, SCC) reproduced this recipe and added their own fixes for the case where the supervised prior is weak: rule-guided distillation at critical states, a trust region to the previous checkpoint instead of to the fixed supervised policy, and "agent branching" that uses z pseudo-rewards to make exploiters cheaply.

### Cited Findings

AlphaStar (Nature 2019):
- Reward: "We use the match outcome (-1 on a loss, 0 on a draw and +1 on a win) as the terminal reward r_T, without a discount to accurately reflect the true goal of winning games." — [Vinyals et al. 2019, Methods "Reinforcement Learning"](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- The paper names the problem directly: "exploration is difficult due to domain complexity and reward sparsity; policies need to be capable of executing diverse strategies throughout training; and off-policy learning is difficult due to large time horizons and the complex action space." — [Vinyals et al. 2019](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- Human data as the exploration device: "First, we initialise the policy parameters to the supervised policy and continually minimise the KL divergence between the supervised and current policy. Second, we train the main agents with pseudo-rewards to follow a strategy statistic z, which we randomly sample from human data. These pseudo-rewards measure the edit distance between sampled and executed build orders, and the Hamming distance between sampled and executed cumulative statistics. Each type of pseudo-reward is active (i.e. non-zero) with probability 25%, and separate value functions and losses are computed for each pseudo-reward. We found our use of human data to be critical in achieving good performance with reinforcement learning (Fig. 3E)." — [Vinyals et al. 2019](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- Definition of z: "we extract a statistic z encoding each player's build order, defined as the first 20 constructed buildings and units, and cumulative statistics, defined as the units, buildings, effects, and upgrades that were present during a game. We condition the policy on z in both supervised and reinforcement learning, and in supervised learning we set z to zero 10% of the time." — [Vinyals et al. 2019](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- During RL the agent is "either condition[ed] on a statistic z, in which case agents receive a reward for following the strategy corresponding to z, or train[ed] unconditionally, in which case the agent is free to choose its own strategy. Agents also receive a penalty whenever their action probabilities differ from the supervised policy." — [Vinyals et al. 2019](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- Ablation Fig. 3E ("Human data usage", Test Elo against Ablation Test Agents, simplified setup: one map Kairos Junction, Protoss vs Protoss, RL limited to 10^10 steps, main agents only, 50/50 self-play and PFSP): No human data 149; Supervised 936; Human init 1020; + Supervised KL 1400; + Statistics 1540. — [Vinyals et al. 2019, Fig. 3](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- Ablation Fig. 3I ("Off-policy learning", average win-rate when training a best response against Fixed Opponents): V-Trace 49%; + TD(lambda) 73%; + UPGO 82%. The hybrid is: "The policy is updated using V-trace and the value estimates are updated using TD(lambda), which does not apply off-policy corrections". — [Vinyals et al. 2019, Fig. 3 and Methods](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- UPGO definition: update in the direction rho_t (G_t^U - V(s_t,z)) grad log pi, where G_t^U bootstraps with r_t + G_{t+1}^U if Q(s_{t+1},a_{t+1},z) >= V(s_{t+1},z) and r_t + V(s_{t+1},z) otherwise; "we estimate action-values with a one-step target, Q(s_t,a_t,z) = r_t + V(s_{t+1},z)". Described as self-imitation: "update the policy from partial trajectories with better-than-expected returns by bootstrapping when the behaviour policy takes a worse-than-average action". — [Vinyals et al. 2019, Methods](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- Ablation Fig. 3K ("Value function", average win-rate, same best-response setup): Without opponent info 22%; With opponent info 82%. "To decrease variance of the value estimates, we also use the opponent's observations as input to the value functions ... these are only used during training, as value functions are unnecessary during evaluation." — [Vinyals et al. 2019](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- Overall loss: "a weighted sum of the policy and value function losses ... corresponding to the win-loss reward r_T as well as pseudo-rewards based on human data, the KL divergence loss with respect to the supervised policy, and the standard entropy regularisation loss." Trace cutting mitigation: "we assume independence between the action type, delay, and all other arguments, and so update them separately." — [Vinyals et al. 2019](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- League composition ablation Fig. 3A/B (Test Elo of main agents; Relative Population Performance of whole league): Main Agents 1540 / 6%; + Main Exploiters 1693 / 35%; + League Exploiters 1824 / 62%. — [Vinyals et al. 2019, Fig. 3](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- Multi-agent algorithm ablation Fig. 3C/D (Test Elo; minimum win-rate against all past versions): FSP 1143 / 69%; pFSP 1273 / 70%; SP 1519 / 46%; pFSP+SP 1540 / 71%. Caption: "Naive self-play has high Elo, but is more forgetful." — [Vinyals et al. 2019, Fig. 3](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- APM ablation Fig. 3G (Test Elo): 0% limit 0; 10% 1145; 25% 1419; 50% 1536; 100% 1540; 200% 1411; no limit 1392. "Surprisingly, increasing APM also reduces performance, possibly because the agent spends more effort refining micro-tactics than learning diverse strategies." — [Vinyals et al. 2019, Fig. 3](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- Architecture ablation Fig. 3F (supervised win-rate vs built-in Elite bot): Baseline 0%; + Action delays 7%; + Pointer network 36%; + Transformer 71%; + Scatter connections 87%. Interface Fig. 3H: camera 87%, non-camera 96%. Built-in bot Elos Fig. 3J: no-op 0, Very Easy 418, Elite 603. — [Vinyals et al. 2019, Fig. 3](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- Extended Data Fig. 5 caption: "PFSP based training outperforms FSP under all measures considered: it has a stronger population measured by relative population performance, provides a less exploitable solution, and has better final agent performance against the corresponding League." — [Vinyals et al. 2019](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- January 2019 version (blog): "Each agent has its own learning objective: for example, which competitors should this agent aim to beat, and any additional internal motivations that bias how the agent plays. One agent may have an objective to beat one specific competitor, while another agent may have to beat a whole distribution of competitors, but do so by building more of a particular game unit." — [DeepMind blog 2019](https://deepmind.google/discover/blog/alphastar-mastering-the-real-time-strategy-game-starcraft-ii/)

TStarBot-X (2020), the faithful small-scale reimplementation:
- Reward follows AlphaStar: "the win-loss outcome, the Edit distance between the build order in z-stat and the immediate build order, and the Manhattan distances between the build units, effects, upgrades in z-stat and the according immediate values. Except the win-loss reward, each of the other rewards is activated with a probability of 0.25 for the main agent. Exploiters initialized from the standard supervised agent only use the win-loss reward. Exploiters initialized from diversely fine-tuned supervised agents use all the above rewards with 25% activation probability on the build order reward and 100% activation for others. The discount factor gamma is set to 1.0". — [Han et al. 2020, sec. 4.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.13729)
- RL loss: L_RL = L_V-trace + L_UPGO + L_entropy + L_distill (distillation toward the supervised model); "distributed PPO + UPGO does not show much difference compared with V-trace + UPGO within 5 days of training." — [Han et al. 2020, sec. 4.6](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.13729)
- What a straight AlphaStar league did at 1/30 the consuming speed and 1/73 the generating speed: "although the league training can consistently improve the agent's performance, the strategic diversity and strength in the entire league is significantly limited. In several repetitions, the main agent converges to either Zergingling Rush or Roach Push (two early Zerg strategies) in a few days ... We conjecture that insufficient explorations prevent the agents from improving as expected." — [Han et al. 2020, sec. 1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.13729)
- Rule-Guided Policy Search (RGPS): L_RGPS = KL(pi_expert(.|s) || pi_theta(.|s)) for s in S_critical, 0 otherwise, where the expert distribution is "an one-hot or multi-hot distribution that is a distributional encoding of 'if-else' logics"; six rules were used (Morph Lair after the opponent does; Morph Overseer, Build Lurkerden after seeing a Lurker; Build Spire/Hydraliskden after seeing air; Train Viper after many Lurkers; Morph Hive if the Viper rule fires); RGPS "is only activated for MA". Motivation: "within one match these key state-action pairs ... would probably only be performed once among thousands of steps." — [Han et al. 2020, sec. 4.6 and Table 6](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.13729)
- DAPO: L_DAPO = KL(pi_{theta_{t-1}} || pi_{theta_t}) between successive stored models (a learning period of about 12 hours), "only activated for the first 4 minutes of an episode". The reason for replacing the fixed supervised anchor: "our supervised agent loses some games when playing with Platinum human players half the time ... consistently restricting MA around the supervised agent prevents MA from improving efficiently." — [Han et al. 2020, sec. 4.7](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.13729)
- Ablation (each arm 7 days at full 144-GPU scale, measured by Relative Population Performance against the "AlphaStar Surrogate" league): "By using DLT, although the entire league's RPP is only slightly higher than AlphaStar Surrogate, the RPP of the MAs increases apparently"; "By adding RGPS, we also observe slight RPP improvement of both the league and MAs"; "after adding DAPO, the RPP values against AlphaStar Surrogate significantly increases for both league and MAs". In the 57-day formal run DAPO was switched on for the last 15 days: "the strength of all the agents is significantly improved after DAPO activated." No numeric RPP values are printed in the text (they are in Figure 6). — [Han et al. 2020, sec. 5.1.2-5.1.3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.13729)

SCC (ICML 2021):
- Loss: "L_RL = L_PPO + L_entropy + L_KL", the KL "with respect to the final supervised policy to prevent RL policy deviating from humans". — [Wang et al. 2021, sec. 6.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.13169)
- z pseudo-rewards adopted: "At the start of each game, we randomly sample a z, and at each following agent step, the rewards are calculated as the distances between each part of the sampled statistic z and the actually executed statistic ... Separate value functions and advantages are computed for each reward, and the generalized advantage estimation (GAE) ... is applied". — [Wang et al. 2021, sec. 6.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.13169)
- Value warm-up: "we freeze the policy network and update only the value network at the first 50 training steps for warm up"; policy and value share weights up to the LSTM output. — [Wang et al. 2021, sec. 6.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.13169)
- Agent branching: "the main agent can play partially according to the specified z but not perfectly ... different z lead to different win rates range between 35% to 65%" against the unconditional main agent; "instead of initializing with the supervised agent and training with sparse win/loss reward, we propose to initialize the new agent with the current main agent and train it with not only the sparse win/loss reward, but also the dense reward based on a specific z". The observed failure it fixes: "it is really difficult for the exploiters to discover and execute similar strategies [reaper rush] very well if only sparse win/loss reward is used." — [Wang et al. 2021, sec. 6.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.13169)
- Result of RL over SL against built-in bots (100 matches each, Triton): supervised TvT 0.97 vs Elite and 0.29 vs CheatInsane; after league RL 1.0 and 1.0 (TvZ CheatInsane 0.99). — [Wang et al. 2021, Tables 1-2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.13169)

TStarBots (2018), flat RL over macro actions with pure win/loss:
- "Ternary valued reward function: 1 (win) / 0 (tie) / -1 (loss) received at the end of a game. The reward is always zero during the game." Learned with Dueling Double DQN and PPO; the value target used a "Mixture of Monte-Carlo (MMC) return with the boostrapped Q-learning return as the Q update target, which accelerates the reward propagation and stabilizes the training." — [Sun et al. 2018 (ar5iv rendering)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1809.07193)
- Frames to reach 75% win-rate (PPO): Easy (L2) 30M; Hard (L4) 250M; Very Hard (L6) 800M; Cheat Resources (L9) 2,000M; Cheat Insane (L10) 3,500M. Final: DDQN 71.8% and PPO 81% vs L10 (200 games each). — [Sun et al. 2018 (ar5iv rendering)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1809.07193)

AlphaStar Unplugged (2023), what the win/loss bit in HUMAN data is worth:
- "The win-loss reward is a weak learning signal to learn a good policy because the dataset has games with a wide range of qualities and the win-loss reward ignores this. For example, the winner of a game between two low-skilled players would consistently lose to the loser of a game between two professional players." — [Mathieu et al. 2023, sec. 6](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.03526)
- Reference agents vs the very_hard built-in bot (robustness / Elo / win rate): BC 25% / 1380 / 88%; FT-BC 36% / 1485 / 95%; MuZero Supervised 30% / 1425 / 92%; MuZero Supervised + MCTS 50% / 1578 / 95%; Offline Actor-Critic 43% / 1548 / 98%; Emphatic OAC 43% / 1563 / 97%. For scale: very_hard bot 3% / 1000 / 50%; AlphaStar Supervised (all races) 8% / 1171 / 75%; race-specific 17% / 1280 / 82%; race-specific + fine-tuning 44% / 1545 / 94%; AlphaStar Final 100% / 2968 / 100%. — [Mathieu et al. 2023, Table 5](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.03526)
- Methods that did NOT work on human data with win/loss: offline Q-learning "very unstable, and they have 0% win rate against the very_hard bot"; CRR, BCQ, CWP, R-BVE "all achieved around 83-84%" (no gain over BC); return-conditioned BC "seemed to ignore the return conditioning". The working ones kept the policy near the behaviour policy: "many recent works have found these algorithms can be made more effective simply by making modifications that ensure the target policy stays close to the behavior policy mu, that the value function stays close to V_mu, or both." — [Mathieu et al. 2023, sec. 5.12 and 6](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.03526)

### Inferences
- Ordering of mechanism value in AlphaStar's own numbers (Elo, ablation setup): supervised init over from-scratch +787; KL anchor over bare init +380; z statistics and their pseudo-rewards +140; main exploiters +153; league exploiters +131. The two RL-internals ablations are on a different scale (win rate as a best response) but are the largest single effects reported: opponent-visible value function 22% to 82%, and the TD(lambda)+UPGO hybrid 49% to 82%.
- Every agent that reached top-human play kept win/loss as the only outcome reward and made the SIGNAL dense through auxiliaries that are all derived from the human corpus (init, KL, z pseudo-rewards, MMR-filtered fine-tuning). None reports a hand-shaped material or score reward in the final recipe.
- The two small-compute reimplementations both found the fixed KL-to-supervised anchor to be a ceiling when the supervised agent is weak (TStarBot-X: Platinum-level SL agent; SCC: exploiters can't find reaper rush) and both added a mechanism that supplies targeted, denser signal (RGPS rules; z-guided branching) rather than a global shaped reward.
- Unplugged's Table 5 gives the cleanest "value of the outcome bit in a human dataset" number: from BC 88% to OAC 98% vs very_hard, with robustness 25% to 43%, using nothing but the replays plus their outcomes. That is a no-self-play route to improving an imitation prior.

### Gaps
- The numeric coefficients of AlphaStar's KL term, the pseudo-reward weights, and the per-pseudo-reward value-loss weights are in the Nature supplementary pseudocode ("pseudocode.zip") and "detailed-architecture.txt", which were not fetched; the main text gives only the 25% activation probability and the 10% z-dropout in supervised learning.
- TStarBot-X's RPP ablation values exist only as a figure (Fig. 6); the text says "slightly" / "apparently" / "significantly" without numbers.
- SCC reports no with/without-KL or with/without-z ablation; the only quantified ablations are supervised-stage ones (dataset size, MMR filter, batch size).

## Key question 2: How were human replays used beyond initial imitation, and what happened without them?

### Takeaway
Replays were used four ways beyond initialisation: (1) as a running KL anchor during RL; (2) as a source of sampled strategy statistics z that condition the policy and generate pseudo-rewards; (3) as MMR- and winner-filtered fine-tuning sets (AlphaStar 87% to 96% vs Elite; SCC's MMR 5300 subset about 10 points over MMR 4300; Unplugged 84% to 89%); (4) as diverse starting points for exploiters (TStarBot-X fine-tuned six strategy-specific supervised models; SCC seeded exploiters from the main agent with a chosen z). Without human data, RL from scratch reached Elo 149 against 1540 with everything (AlphaStar Fig. 3E), and SC2LE's baselines "do not learn to win even a single game" on the full game; a small-scale AlphaStar league without diversity mechanisms collapsed to two rush strategies. Importance sampling of rare actions in imitation (TStarBot-X, 68% to 85% vs Elite) is the one imitation-stage change with a large reported effect.

### Cited Findings
- AlphaStar supervised data: "971,000 replays played on StarCraft II versions 4.8.2 to 4.8.6 by players with MMR ... greater than 3500, i.e. from the top 22% of players"; "We further fine-tune the policy using only winning replays with MMR above 6200 (16,000 games). Fine-tuning improved the win-rate against the built-in Elite bot from 87% to 96% in Protoss versus Protoss. The fine-tuned supervised agents were rated at 3947, 3607, 3544 MMR for Terran, Protoss, and Zerg". The supervised agent alone "reached an average rating of 3,699, which places it above 84% of human players". — [Vinyals et al. 2019](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- Why human data and not exploration: "It is highly improbable that naive exploration will execute a precise sequence of instructions, over thousands of steps, that constructs air units and effectively utilizes their micro-tactics." — [Vinyals et al. 2019](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- Validation agents were built by fixing z: "a set of 17 strategies trained using only main agents and no exploiters, and fixing z to a hand-curated set of interesting strategies (e.g. a cannon rush or early flying units)". — [Vinyals et al. 2019, Methods "Analysis"](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- The paper's own generality caveats: "Imitation learning in AlphaStar requires a large amount of human demonstrations to be effective ... Using a latent variable z to induce exploration is not specific to StarCraft, but the particular choice of statistics required domain knowledge." — [Vinyals et al. 2019, "AlphaStar Generality"](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- 2019 blog version: the supervised agent "defeated the built-in 'Elite' level AI - around gold level for a human player - in 95% of games." — [DeepMind blog 2019](https://deepmind.google/discover/blog/alphastar-mastering-the-real-time-strategy-game-starcraft-ii/)
- Without human data on the full game (2017): "Notably, baseline agents do not learn to win even a single game" with the win/loss reward; with the Blizzard score reward agents "converged to simply preserving the initial mining process without building further units or structures"; "current state-of-the-art baseline agents cannot learn to win against the easiest built-in AI on the full game". Supervised learning from 800K replays gave 64% value-prediction accuracy and 37.9% top-1 action-identifier accuracy. — [Vinyals et al. 2017 (ar5iv rendering)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1708.04782)
- TStarBot-X data and importance sampling: 309,571 ZvZ replays with at least one player MMR >= 3500; "Without importance sampling, i.e., using the original data from human replays, the win-rate against the Elite-bot only reaches 68%"; the fix: "we downsample 'NOOP' by a rate of 0.2 and downsample 'Smart' by a rate of 0.25, while all other abilities that fail to appear in each replay at least once in average will be upsampled by the ratio the number of total replays over the ability's total count, capped by 10.0", weights reassigned to the loss so the estimate stays unbiased; this "will increase the win-rate of the agent against the Elite-bot from 68% to 85% (90% win-rate mentioned in introduction is achieved by further fine-tuning the model on data with higher MMR)", the fine-tune being on "1500 human replays with at least one human player's MMR above 6200". — [Han et al. 2020, sec. 4.4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.13729)
- TStarBot-X z-stats: "a subset of 174 z-stats collected from human replays" with "the winner's MMR >= 6200 on the Kairos Junction map"; a z-stat "includes a build order sequence of length 20, the coordinate of each unit in that order and a vector indicating the unit appearance in the sampled replay". Human evaluation used "zero z-stat, with 50% probability and randomly sample z-stat from the top-25 z-stats". — [Han et al. 2020, sec. 4.2, 5.1.1, 5.2.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.13729)
- TStarBot-X diversity from replays: "we first fine-tune the supervised agent on K separate sub-datasets, in each of which some specific units or strategies take place in human replays" (K = 6: Hydralisk, Lurker, Mutalisk, Nydus Network, TunnelingClaws, Worker Rush), used as initial models for Specific Exploiters; "SE only uses the win-loss reward and samples z-stats from the according subset of human replays. It utilizes policy distillation to stay close to its specific initial policy with a larger coefficient compared to others." — [Han et al. 2020, sec. 4.4-4.5](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.13729)
- SCC data: 105,034 replays (versions 4.10.0-4.11.2, MMR > 4,300; TvT 34,487, TvP 39,391, TvZ 31,156). Dataset-size ablation: with 4,638, 15,579 and 105,034 replays "the three resulting models perform quite similar, with peak win rates all slightly above 70%" vs Elite (batch 3,850), the small one overfitting after its peak; MMR-5300 subset (17,173 replays) "peak performance is around 85%, about 10% higher than the MMR4300 dataset"; doubling batch size to 7,700 "improved ... both the learning speed and the peak win rate"; final recipe: full dataset at batch 15,400, then fine-tune on "MMR5500-win dataset ... of size 3,458 ... only the winning replays with MMR score above 5,500" at lr 1e-5, reaching "97% consistently" vs Elite. — [Wang et al. 2021, sec. 5](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.13169)
- Unplugged filtering table (BC, win rate vs very_hard): main training on MMR > 3500 win+loss (2,776,466 episodes) 84%; MMR > 6000 win+loss (64,894) 65%; MMR > 6000 wins only (32,447) 51%; MMR > 3500 then fine-tune on MMR > 6200 wins (21,836) 89%. "restricting the training set to fewer, higher quality episodes results in poorer performance. However, training using the full dataset followed by a fine-tuning phase on high quality data works best". Also: "the win rate with only 10% of the episodes in the dataset is close to the best one". — [Mathieu et al. 2023, Table 2 and sec. 5.4-5.5](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.03526)
- Unplugged MMR conditioning: "the MMR of the player who generated the trajectory is passed as a vector input. During inference, we can control the quality of the game played by the agent by changing the MMR input. In practice, we set the MMR to the highest value". — [Mathieu et al. 2023, sec. 4.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.03526)
- Unplugged memory finding (behaviour cloning vs very_hard): LSTM 70%, no memory 84%, Transformer 85% (10^10 frames) and 89% (2x10^10). — [Mathieu et al. 2023, Table 3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.03526)
- DI-star (Zerg): "160k human replays(mmr above 4000, versions from 4.8.2 to 4.9.3) are used for training. 7k replays (mmr above 5800) are used for fine-tuning"; "After 165k iterations (1s per iteration, totally 2 days) of training, the agent has a 87% win rate against built-in elite bot. After 37k iterations of fine-tuning, the win rate reaches 97%." Z (strategy statistic) files are shipped under the library directory. — [DI-star small-scale guide](https://github.com/opendilab/DI-star/blob/main/docs/guidance_to_small_scale_training.md)
- Brood War, imitation of macromanagement (2017): 2,005 PvT replays, 789,571 state-action pairs, predicting the next build; top-1 error 54.6%, top-3 error 22.9%; plugged into UAlbertaBot it won 68% vs the built-in Terran bot and 45% vs UAlbertaBot's fixed marine rush; "the best hand-crafted strategies are still the state-of-the-art". — [Justesen and Risi 2017 (ar5iv rendering)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1707.03743)
- Mini-AlphaStar: "We find the mAS agent with randomly initialed parameters can hardly get positive signals in training. E.g., the agent randomly chooses actions (most of these can't be executed due to their preconditions being not satisfied) at the early age of the training and can't do anything for a long time"; SL from 60 self-generated replays on Simple64 reached "a 0.15 win rate against the level-1 built-in AI". — [Liu et al. 2022, sec. 5](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.11553)

### Inferences
- The winner-and-high-rating fine-tune is the most consistently reproduced replay-derived gain across four independent groups (DeepMind twice, inspir.ai, Tencent, OpenDILab), each worth roughly 5-10 points of Elite-bot win rate on top of a full-corpus model. Filtering the whole corpus to high ratings instead loses (Unplugged 84% to 65%) because it loses coverage.
- z serves two functions that are separable: diversity/exploration control (AlphaStar, TStarBot-X) and a cheap way to manufacture specialised opponents (SCC branching, TStarBot-X specific exploiters). The second function is the one a small budget can afford, since it replaces an exploiter's search for a strategy with a distance-to-statistic reward.
- Importance sampling toward rare actions (TStarBot-X) is the imitation-stage analogue of "act more": the downsampled classes were the do-nothing and the generic "Smart" click.

### Gaps
- No paper reports a controlled "RL with human init but no replays afterward, at small compute" run other than AlphaStar's Fig. 3E, which is at DeepMind scale (10^10 steps) and PvP on one map.
- DI-star's README claims master/grandmaster strength but the repository gives no match record, MMR or opponent list beyond a video against one player; treat the strength claim as unverified.

## Key question 3: Macro-action and hierarchical decompositions: design, sample efficiency, learned vs hand-designed

### Takeaway
Two families exist. Hand-designed macro actions (TStarBots: 165 for Zerg, with hard-coded preconditions, technology-tree and placement rules; LastOrder: 54 for Brood War Zerg; Lee 2018: build-order and tactics modules over scripted micro) let flat RL with pure win/loss beat the cheating built-in AI, at a cost of thousands of CPUs or hand-written micro. Data-mined macro actions (Pang 2019 / Liu 2022: PrefixSpan over expert replays, top 75 frequent sequences, "reduced the number of actions from hundreds to dozens") combined with a two- then three-level hierarchy trained on one 4-GPU/48-core machine, reaching 93% vs level 7 in the AAAI version and 96/97/94% vs the cheating levels 8/9/10 in the JAIR version. The reported sample-efficiency evidence is indirect: a random policy over the mined macro actions wins 3% vs level 7 (0% with atomic actions), and the same machine running a scaled-down AlphaStar over 564 raw actions for 3 days reached 0.74 vs level 1 and 0.00 vs level 7 against the hierarchy's 1.00 and 0.93. Only the Brood War build-order switcher (Gehring 2018) learned its high-level choice from outcome-labelled self-play at scale (2.8M games); the macro actions themselves were never learned end-to-end in any of these systems.

### Cited Findings
- TStarBots macro action space: 165 macro actions for Zerg in five categories: Building 13 (rules: TechTree, RandUnit, RandPlace), Production 22, Tech Upgrading 27, Resources Harvesting 3, Combating 100 ("nine zones (A-I) plus one global zone (J), creating 10x10=100 zone-to-zone attack configurations"). TStarBot1 = flat RL over these; TStarBot2 = rule-based hierarchy, 90% vs L10 "with no reinforcement learning component". — [Sun et al. 2018 (ar5iv rendering)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1809.07193)
- TStarBots' precondition trick, as described by Liu et al.: "Suppose the agent finds the action needed to be executed can't be taken (maybe the precondition is not satisfied). In that case, the agent will then instead perform the precondition action. This trick gives the agent more opportunity to perform an executable action. However, this trick needs human knowledge." Compute contrast: "Sun's work uses 3840 CPU cores, while ours uses 48 ones." — [Liu et al. 2022, sec. 4.5](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.11553)
- Pang/Liu hierarchy: "the controller decides to choose a sub-policy based on current observation every long time interval, and the sub-policy picks a macro-action every short time interval" (K = 8 s controller, 1 s sub-policy); sub-policies are a base (economy/production) network and a battle policy in three variants (combat rule, combat network, mixture). — [Pang et al. 2019 (ar5iv rendering)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1809.09095)
- Macro-action mining: PrefixSpan on expert trajectories split into 5-second fragments, keep the top 75 patterns subject to no repeated actions, length > 2, first action of select type; "reduced the number of actions from hundreds to dozens"; replays were 30 games played by the authors' experts (Appendix B). — [Liu et al. 2022 (ar5iv rendering and sec. 3.2 / Appendix B)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2209.11553)
- The macro-action baseline number: "The random sampling win rate for the agent on difficulty level-7 is 0.03 using macro actions (the win rate is 0.00 without the macro actions) ... the macro action can only give the agent a very small win rate (can not get win rate above 0.10). It is the usage of our training method to make the agent grow the win rate from a very low one of 0.03 to a very high one of 0.93". — [Liu et al. 2022, sec. 4.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.11553)
- Hierarchy vs flat: "Hierarchical significantly better on level-7; comparable on level-2" (Figure 6a); and the JAIR text notes the flat-with-macro-actions variant "be almost as good as the hierarchical architecture, which means that macro actions are" the main lever at low difficulty (line 1176-1179 of the text; the sentence is cut in the extracted PDF). — [Liu et al. 2022 (ar5iv rendering)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2209.11553)
- Alternate vs simultaneous updating of the sub-networks: on level 7 "the blue line starting with a win rate of 0.2 is very hard to grow beyond the win rate of 0.4 in the later training process ... The green line [alternate updating] grows quickly from the 0.4 win rate to a 0.8 win rate". — [Liu et al. 2022, sec. 4.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.11553)
- Win rates (Table 1, AAAI version; Protoss vs Terran, Simple64, Zealot/Stalker only, 15-minute limit, trained on level 7): combat rule 1.00/0.99/0.94/0.99/0.95/0.88/0.78/0.70/0.73/0.60 for levels 1-10; combat network 0.98/0.99/0.45/0.47/0.39/0.73/0.66/0.56/0.52/0.41; mixture 1.00/1.00/0.99/0.97/1.00/0.90/0.93/0.74/0.71/0.43. — [Pang et al. 2019 (ar5iv rendering, Table 1)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1809.09095)
- Three-layer extension (JAIR 2022): a building sub-network and a population sub-network under the base sub-policy; trained from scratch on each cheating level with the win/loss reward; best iterations gave 0.79 (level 8, iteration 188), 0.87 (level 9, 213 iterations), 0.86 (level 10, 636 iterations) against the 2-layer 0.74/0.71/0.43; the "final 3-layer" hierarchy with a battle-to-population control line plus best practices reached 0.96/0.97/0.94. — [Liu et al. 2022, sec. 6, Tables 7-8](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.11553)
- Lee et al. 2018 modular agent: worker management and micromanagement scripted; build order = "FC policy" choosing unit/building type and, for units, a quantity from {1, 2, 4, 8, 16}, acting "once every 5 seconds"; tactics = "FCN policy"; scouting = scripted + LSTM prediction. Training: A3C with "18 parallel CPU workers", self-play against a pool initialised "with a random agent and other scripted modular agents", snapshots "every 3x10^6 policy steps". Iterative training of modules beat joint training 87% vs 35% against the Harder bot. — [Lee et al. 2018 (ar5iv rendering)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1811.03555)
- LastOrder (Brood War Zerg, AIIDE 2019): 54 macro actions (7 production, 14 building, 15 upgrading, 17 attack, 1 expansion, 1 wait), Ape-X DQN with LSTM, hard-coded micro; AIIDE 2017 offline evaluation 83.06% win rate, "outperforming 26 bots in total 28 entrants"; AIIDE 2018 live: 11th of 25. — [Xu et al. 2019 (ar5iv rendering)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1812.00336)
- Brood War learned build-order switching (FAIR, 2018): 25 hand-written build orders; "a corpus of 2.8M games with 3.3M switches" collected by "random build order switches during a game ... a random switch every 8, 10 or 13 minutes on average"; a Q-function "trained with the win/lose outcome of the game as target" plus an auxiliary opponent-unit-count prediction; switch only "if its value has a minimum advantage of 0.01 over the current active one"; win rates: control 0.793 / 0.387 / 0.556 (Locutus / McRave / combined) vs LSTM value+aux 0.888 / 0.600 / 0.725; initial build orders "chosen according to a bandit algorithm". — [Gehring et al. 2018 (ar5iv rendering)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1811.08568)
- Hierarchical RL with human-chosen subgoals (Xu, Huang et al. 2020) is on two SC2 minigames only, not the full game. — [arXiv 2008.03444](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.03444)

### Inferences
- The sample-efficiency argument for macro actions rests on (a) a non-zero random baseline (3% vs 0%) that gives the outcome reward something to grip, and (b) the mAS comparison on identical hardware (3 days: 0.00 vs 0.93 at level 7). Neither is a controlled ablation of "same policy, macro vs atomic"; the closest is Liu 2022's flat-with-macro-actions arm, which they report as nearly as good as the hierarchy.
- Every published macro-action set encodes preconditions or targets (TStarBots' tech tree and zone attacks; PrefixSpan sequences of already-legal operations). What was learned from data was which sequences humans use, not what a macro action should do.
- Gehring's switcher is the only case where a high-level choice was fit purely to outcomes: 2.8M self-generated games with random switches, i.e., a randomised experiment on the high-level decision, which is why a value target on win/loss was enough.

### Gaps
- No paper isolates the contribution of macro actions with matched compute and the same learner; Pang/Liu's reward, hierarchy and curriculum comparisons are all on the macro-action space.
- Lee 2018's total compute (games, days) is not stated in the paper.

## Key question 4: Compute of the cheapest full-game SC2 agents, and what they reached

### Takeaway
The cheapest published full-game RL agent is Pang/Liu's hierarchical PPO on one machine with 4 GPUs and 48 CPU cores (50 concurrent games, 100 games per 4-8 minute iteration, 2-3 days per run; 636 iterations for the level-10 result, on the order of 60,000 games), reaching 93% vs the top non-cheating built-in AI and 94% vs the top cheating one, on a 64x64 map with a restricted unit set. The same hardware running a scaled-down AlphaStar for 3 days reached 0.74 vs level 1 and 0.00 vs level 7. Lee 2018 used 18 CPU workers and reached 57-60% vs Very Hard. Beating strong humans has not been done below the 144-GPU / 13,440-CPU / 57-day scale (TStarBot-X) or SCC's 1,000 concurrent environments per agent for 58 days; AlphaStar used 12 agents each on 32 TPU v3 for 44 days with 16,000 concurrent games per agent, after a 14-day, 16-TPU-per-agent first version.

### Cited Findings
- Pang/Liu machine and rates: "a single machine with 4 GPUs and 48 CPU threads"; "10 workers x 5 threads = 50 SC2 environments"; "100 full-length games per iteration"; "4 to 8 minutes for one iteration"; "a full run of SC2 training needs 2 to 3 days". Level-10 result at iteration 636 (Table 7). — [Liu et al. 2022 (ar5iv rendering and sec. 4.2 / 6.1)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2209.11553)
- Same-hardware comparison (Table 6; Terran built-in AI, Simple64, 100 games x 3 repeats): mAS SL 0.3 day: 0.15 / 0.07 / 0.02 / 0.00 vs levels 1 / 2 / 3 / 7; mAS RL 1 day: 0.54 / 0.30 / 0.02 / 0.00; mAS RL 3 days: 0.74 / 0.51 / 0.08 / 0.00; hierarchical RL 3 days: 1.00 / 1.00 / 0.99 / 0.93. mAS used only the win/loss reward in this comparison. — [Liu et al. 2022, Table 6 and sec. 5](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.11553)
- mAS scale-down (Table 1 of the report): batch 96 (vs 512), LSTM hidden 128 (vs 384), 1 LSTM layer (vs 3), 4 resblocks (vs 16), embedding size 1543 (vs 3585), 4 league learners (vs 12), 512 actor loops (vs 16,000); "the final performance of the training is not ideal". — [Liu et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06890)
- TStarBot1: "1∼2 days of training with a single GPU and 3840 CPUs" (1,920 actors on 80 machines, about 16,000 frames/s), trained against a mixture of difficulty levels 1, 2, 4, 6, 9, 10; 3,500M frames to 75% vs level 10. — [Sun et al. 2018 (ar5iv rendering)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1809.07193)
- Lee 2018: A3C with 18 parallel CPU workers; win rates (Table 4) Hard 84±5%, Harder 87±3%, Very Hard 57±7%, Elite 31±11%; with fog of war (Table 7) 95±1 / 94±2 / 50±8 / 60±8. — [Lee et al. 2018 (ar5iv rendering)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1811.03555)
- TStarBot-X vs AlphaStar (Table 1): GPUs 144 V100 (96 training, 48 inference) vs 3,072 TPU v3 cores; CPUs 13,440 vs 50,400 preemptible; concurrent matches 6,720 vs 192,000; 57 vs 44 days; consuming 210 fps/GPU vs 390 fps/TPU core; "AlphaStar's total consuming/generating speed in the league is about 30/73 times faster". Formal run: "25,708,491 matches ... 583 agents ... including 124 main agents, 452 exploiters and 7 initial supervised models"; MA on 32 GPUs, each exploiter on 8; imitation on 144 GPUs and 5,600 CPU cores for 43 hours; policy 17.25M + value 2.75M parameters vs AlphaStar's 139M. Human results: Masters 11:0 and 13:0; Grandmasters 3:0, 4:1, 4:0. — [Han et al. 2020, Tables 1 and 5, sec. 5](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.13729)
- SCC: "For each agent, 1000 StarCraft environments are used to collect samples (16,000 for AlphaStar) and the learner consumes about 800 agent steps per second (50,000 for AlphaStar). In total, each agent experienced 30 years of real-time StarCraft play (200 game years for AlphaStar)"; 49M parameters; a sample batch of 144,000 per 180-second training step; main agent 1 trained about 58 days, main agents' Elo above 1,500 vs the supervised agent at 0. Test matches: Diamond 5:0 (day 23), Master 4:1 (day 30), Grandmaster 3:2 (day 39), Grandmaster 5:0 (day 47); live 2:0 vs TIME and 2:0 vs TooDming on 21 June 2020. — [Wang et al. 2021, sec. 3, 6.3, 7 and Appendix B](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.13169)
- AlphaStar: "Each agent was trained using 32 third-generation tensor processing units (TPUs) over 44 days. During league training almost 900 distinct players were created."; "we run 16,000 concurrent StarCraft II matches and 16 actor tasks (each using a TPU v3 device with 8 TPU cores)"; CPUs "roughly equivalent to 150 processors with 28 physical cores each"; a "128-core TPU learner ... total batch size of 512 ... about 50,000 agent steps per second", data "replayed twice"; 12 actor-learner copies. — [Vinyals et al. 2019, Methods "Infrastructure"](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- AlphaStar v1: "The AlphaStar league was run for 14 days, using 16 TPUs for each agent"; "each agent experienced up to 200 years of real-time StarCraft play". — [DeepMind blog 2019](https://deepmind.google/discover/blog/alphastar-mastering-the-real-time-strategy-game-starcraft-ii/)
- DI-star large scale: supervised "56 Nvidia A100 graphics cards for learner and 1792 cpu cores for replay_actor" (batch 336, trajectory 64); RL "128 Nvidia A100 for 4 learners (each with 32) and approximately 10000 cpu cores for 300 actors", batch 192 per learner, "1.5s per iteration", "2 months (2200k iterations)". Small scale: "Only 9000 zerg-vs-zerg replays (276MB) are required", "32G RAM, 8G GPU" (a 3070), default batch 2 / trajectory 32; evaluation is against the level-3 bot and no small-scale win rate is given. — [DI-star small-scale guide](https://github.com/opendilab/DI-star/blob/main/docs/guidance_to_small_scale_training.md)
- Unplugged compute: reference agents were trained on about 10^10 observations (FT-BC and the actor-critics on 11 billion frames in total; BC, MZS and MZS-MCTS on 10^10); "although offline RL improves upon the behavior cloning baseline, they are far from the online RL performance of AlphaStar Final, which was trained using several orders of magnitude more computing power." — [Mathieu et al. 2023, sec. 3.2, 5.10](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.03526)

### Inferences
- In games rather than dollars, the single-machine hierarchical agent used on the order of 10^4-10^5 full games per run (100 per iteration, 188-636 iterations for the cheating-level results); the 2018 flat macro-action agent needed 3.5x10^9 frames; the human-beating agents needed 10^7 games (TStarBot-X: 25.7M). A budget of tens of dollars of single-GPU time is at the bottom of the first band [project context].
- What cheap compute bought was "beat the built-in AI on a small map with a restricted unit set", not "beat humans"; the only human-level results below AlphaStar's scale still used more than a hundred GPUs for two months.

### Gaps
- Pang 2019 / Liu 2022 do not state total game counts explicitly; the 60,000-game figure above is derived from 100 games per iteration times the reported iteration counts.
- Lee 2018 gives no wall-clock or game count.
- DI-star's small-scale setting has no reported result.

## Key question 5: Which ideas are specific to real-time / imperfect-information play, and which carry to a turn-based hex wargame with an exact simulator?

### Takeaway
Real-time-specific: APM limits, the delay/NOOP head, camera interface, LSTM memory over 15,000-step episodes, importance sampling against "NOOP"/"Smart" spam, and the whole model-free stance (AlphaStar "sidesteps the difficulties of search-based methods due to imperfect models"). Transferable with reported effect sizes: initialise from imitation and keep a KL anchor (+380 Elo in AlphaStar's ablation); train the value function on privileged (opponent-visible) state during training only (22% to 82%); winner/high-rating fine-tuning of the imitation prior (+5 to +10 points in four groups); offline actor-critic on the human corpus with its outcomes (BC 88% to 98% vs very_hard with no self-play); z-style strategy statistics as conditioning and as a cheap way to build specialised opponents; PFSP over a checkpoint pool to avoid the forgetting that plain self-play shows (46% vs 71% minimum win rate against the past). Shaped rewards have a consistent record: they help against weak opponents or early in training and are dropped or fail at the top (SC2LE score reward degenerates; Pang's designed reward lost to win/loss at cheating levels; Lee's supply-difference reward plateaued at Very Hard). The transfers marked speculative below have no Wesnoth-side measurement.

### Cited Findings
- Real-time constraints that shaped the SC2 designs: "Agents are limited to executing at most 22 non-duplicate actions per five second window"; "agents decide ahead of time when to observe next (on average 370 ms, but possibly multiple seconds)"; tens of thousands of time-steps per game. — [Vinyals et al. 2019, Methods](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- Model-free by necessity: "AlphaStar proposes a model free, end-to-end learning approach to playing StarCraft II which sidesteps the difficulties of search-based methods due to imperfect models". — [Vinyals et al. 2019, Related Work](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- Explicitly general components per the authors: "Using an opponent's observations for a lower-variance baseline and new components, such as hybrid off-policy learning, UPGO, and distillation towards an imitation policy, are also widely applicable"; "we propose a new multi-agent training regime with different kinds of exploiters ... Together with prioritised FSP, these are all general-purpose techniques applicable to any multiplayer domain." — [Vinyals et al. 2019, "AlphaStar Generality"](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- Search on top of an imitation policy, offline: MuZero Supervised 92% vs very_hard and robustness 30%; with MCTS at inference 95% and 50%; Elo 1425 to 1578 (Table 5). — [Mathieu et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.03526)
- Shaped rewards, the record:
  - SC2LE 2017: Blizzard-score agents "converged to simply preserving the initial mining process without building further units or structures". — [Vinyals et al. 2017 (ar5iv rendering)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1708.04782)
  - Pang/Liu: "The win/loss rewards can achieve similar results on low difficulty levels as the handcrafted reward. However, when training is on high difficulty levels, we find that the performance of win/loss reward on the hierarchical model is relatively poor" (level 7, 2-layer); Blizzard score "and winning rate are sometimes not in a proportional positive relationship ... When the agent tries to improve the score, it may ignore the attack chance"; their designed reward compares unit counts to expert-replay averages, adds a time penalty (weight 10) and the outcome; then at cheating levels: "on the cheating difficulty levels, we find that this result reward can get better results, though the learning at first is slower ... both the 3-layer hierarchy and win/loss reward are important"; "In the final 3-layer hierarchical architecture, we use the win/loss (outcome) reward as the reward for RL training." — [Liu et al. 2022, sec. 3.4, 4.4.2, 6.1, Appendix E](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.11553)
  - Lee 2018: "the reward is the change in supply difference r_t = d_t - d_{t-1} for each time step. Summing up all rewards yields a total reward equal to the end-game supply difference", chosen because "win-loss provides too sparse training signals"; ceiling 57-60% vs Very Hard. — [Lee et al. 2018 (ar5iv rendering)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1811.03555)
  - LastOrder (Brood War): terminal reward replaced by "r_new = gamma^timedecay x (score_our - score_enemy) / max(score_our, score_enemy)"; ablation (24 h, 1,000 actors): "Without reward shaping: 0% win-rate against Ximp". — [Xu et al. 2019 (ar5iv rendering)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1812.00336)
  - Pang 2019 also shows the steering power of a shaping term: "if we add rewards for the agent to building Stalkers (one Stalker gives a +5 reward), the agent can learn a tactic of assembling many Stalkers". — [Liu et al. 2022, sec. 4.3 (Fig. 5b)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.11553)
- Curriculum from built-in AI: training "level-1 -> 2 -> 5 -> 7 sequentially, using earlier models as initialization"; "pre-training on levels 2->5 before level-7 training substantially outperforms training from scratch on level-7 directly"; but across a rule change "The training effect based on the previous model is not good due to the randomness of the cheating difficulty level built-in AI ... transfer learning is not good. Therefore, we tried to train from scratch". TStarBot1 instead mixed levels 1, 2, 4, 6, 9, 10 per rollout. — [Pang et al. 2019 (ar5iv rendering)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1809.09095); [Liu et al. 2022, sec. 6.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.11553); [Sun et al. 2018 (ar5iv rendering)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1809.07193)
- League bookkeeping that is domain-independent: main agents "35% SP, 50% PFSP against all past players in the League, and an additional 15% of PFSP matches against forgotten main players"; snapshot "Every 2x10^9 steps"; PFSP weights f_hard(x) = (1-x)^p ("no games are played against opponents that the agent already beats") and f_var(x) = x(1-x) for "main exploiters and struggling main agents"; league exploiters snapshot when they beat everyone >70% or at 2x10^9 steps and reset with 25% probability; main exploiters reset always after 4x10^9 steps. — [Vinyals et al. 2019, Methods "Populating the League"](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- TStarBot-X on why the AlphaStar league schedule does not shrink: "keeping the same amount of periodic training steps for the exploiters costs us more than 10 days for a single period ... using less training steps leads to weak ME and LE agents that can hardly catch up with the continuously trained MA"; their fix (Adaptive Evolutionary Exploiter) inherits a historical exploiter "whose win-rate against MA lies in 20%-50%". — [Han et al. 2020, sec. 4.5](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.13729)
- Non-transitivity is mostly among exploiters: "across the full payoff, there are around 3,000,000 rock-paper-scissor cycles ... that involve at least one exploiter, and around 200 that involve only main agents." — [Vinyals et al. 2019, Extended Data Fig. 8](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- The Brood War switcher's data-generation trick (random high-level switches during self-play, outcome as target) is the one place a high-level decision was learned from win/loss at scale: 2.8M games, combined win rate 0.556 to 0.725. — [Gehring et al. 2018 (ar5iv rendering)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1811.08568)

### Inferences (each tagged)
- Directly transferable, with a number behind it: KL-to-prior during RL (AlphaStar +380 Elo; used by TStarBot-X, SCC, mAS). TStarBot-X's caveat applies if the Wesnoth seed is weak relative to what RL can reach: switch the anchor to the previous checkpoint (DAPO-style, early game only) once the fixed anchor binds. [Speculative on effect size in Wesnoth.]
- Directly transferable, with a number behind it: a value function that sees the full state (both sides, fog lifted) during training only. The Wesnoth simulator has god view, and the project's own principle forbids god view only in the policy's legality mask, not in a training-time critic. AlphaStar's 22% to 82% is the largest single-mechanism effect in the whole literature surveyed. [Speculative on effect size; cheap to test.]
- Transferable without any self-play: offline actor-critic on the 17,000 human games with their outcomes, policy kept close to the imitation policy (Unplugged: BC 88% to OAC 98% vs very_hard; Q-learning 0%; return-conditioning useless). Unplugged's warning about mixed-quality outcomes is the operative risk; MMR conditioning or a rating filter for the fine-tune stage is their mitigation. [Speculative; the strongest-evidenced no-compute route.]
- Transferable: winner-and-high-rating fine-tune of the imitation seed (four independent reproductions, +5 to +10 points vs Elite; Unplugged 84% to 89%). Filtering the whole corpus instead loses. [Cheap; speculative on size.]
- Transferable: importance sampling in imitation toward rare action classes and away from the dominant "do nothing" class (TStarBot-X 68% to 85% vs Elite). The Wesnoth analogue is the end_turn class; the project's own +229 Elo from an end_turn logit offset [project context] is the same lever applied at decode time. [Speculative for the imitation-stage version.]
- Transferable in form, domain-specific in content: z statistics. The SC2 z is "first 20 built units/buildings" plus a presence vector; a Wesnoth z would be the first N recruits and the set of unit types recruited, with edit and Hamming distances as pseudo-rewards, and a config-selected z would force an opener, which is one of the project's stated customisation goals. Its two uses are conditioning for diversity (AlphaStar +140 Elo) and cheap exploiter construction (SCC branching). [Speculative; AlphaStar notes "the particular choice of statistics required domain knowledge".]
- Transferable: PFSP over a checkpoint pool with the f_hard weighting, and one exploiter role. The measured gains (SP forgetting 46% vs 71%; exploiters +153/+131 Elo) came at DeepMind scale; TStarBot-X shows that exploiters that cannot catch the main agent in a short period are wasted, so at tens-of-dollars scale a single continually-trained exploiter (AEE-style inheritance) is the affordable form. [Speculative.]
- Transferable in form: macro actions mined from replays by frequent-sequence mining (PrefixSpan over per-turn action sequences: capture village, retreat to heal, focus-fire). The evidence is that they let a sparse outcome reward register at all (random 3% vs 0%) and that a hierarchy over them trained on one machine. The project's turn-level search is already a two-level decomposition; a data-mined turn macro set would be the sub-policy layer. [Speculative; no controlled ablation exists even in SC2.]
- Transferable: curriculum against a graded scripted opponent, but only within one rule set (Pang/Liu; transfer across the cheating boundary failed). Wesnoth has the RCA AI only through the live bridge and the sim's neutral AI is stationary-combat-only [project context], so this transfer is blocked until a scripted opponent exists in the sim. [Blocked.]
- Not transferable: APM limits, delay/NOOP heads, camera economy, LSTM memory tuned for 15,000-step episodes (Unplugged even found no-memory BC beat LSTM BC, 84% vs 70%), and the 1,000-16,000-concurrent-environment infrastructure numbers.
- Search: the SC2 literature offers no positive evidence for search over an exact model because none had one; the nearest datum is offline MuZero's MCTS at inference (+3 points vs very_hard, robustness 30% to 50%), i.e., modest but positive when the model is learned. The project's finding that a 32-simulation search over ~350 atomic actions does not beat the prior [project context] is consistent with AlphaStar's view that exploration over thousands of atomic steps is hopeless and that the leverage sits at the strategy-statistic level; the SC2 answer to "which level to search or shape" was the build order / unit mix, not the atomic action. [Inference.]
- Shaped rewards: the record says use them, if at all, as a temporary curriculum crutch against weak opponents and drop them once the outcome signal registers; the two agents that beat the top built-in AI at small compute both ended on pure win/loss (TStarBot1 from the start; Liu 2022 after abandoning the designed reward), and the project's own eval contract (no material valuation in evaluation) [project context] already matches this.

### Gaps
- No StarCraft paper measures any of these mechanisms at a budget of tens of GPU-hours; the smallest controlled setting is 4 GPUs for 2-3 days (Pang/Liu) and its comparisons are on a 64x64 map with two unit types.
- No paper reports a KL-anchor coefficient sweep or the point at which the anchor becomes a ceiling; TStarBot-X's switch to DAPO was an unrepeated empirical choice at day 42 of 57.
- No published SC2 agent used an exact forward model, so nothing in this literature speaks to search-versus-policy-gradient trade-offs with a bit-exact simulator.
- The UCB1 claim for CherryPi's opening selection came from a search-result summary of the TorchCraftAI documentation and was not verified against the page.
