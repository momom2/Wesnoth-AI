# Credit assignment and return decomposition for long-horizon, sparse-reward RL, and their fit to a two-player hex wargame with an exact simulator and 17,000 human games

Scope note. Primary sources were read in full text (arXiv/PDF extractions) unless marked otherwise. Numbers quoted are the papers' own. "Speculative transfer" marks an inference about the Wesnoth setting (two-player, turn-based, bit-exact forkable simulator, ~300 atomic decisions per side per game, binary/capped outcome, 17,019 human games with outcomes and ratings, tens of dollars per experiment) that no source tested.

## Key question 1: For each method, what problem structure does it need, what does it cost, what gain did it report on sparse-reward tasks, and which were tested on two-player or adversarial games?

### Takeaway
Every dedicated "credit assignment" method surveyed (RUDDER, Align-RUDDER, HCA, CCA, TVT, Synthetic Returns) was validated only on single-agent tasks, mostly small (Key-to-Door variants, chains, bandits) plus a handful of delayed-reward Atari games; none reports a two-player or self-play result. The methods that have produced strong two-player agents (ExIt/AlphaZero/KataGo, AlphaStar, OpenAI Five) rely on search targets, dense shaped or auxiliary targets, and human-data anchors rather than on return decomposition; the multi-agent counterfactual baseline (COMA) is the one per-unit credit method with a StarCraft result, and it is cooperative-vs-scripted, not self-play.

### Cited Findings

**Return decomposition: RUDDER (Arjona-Medina et al., NeurIPS 2019).**
- Mechanism: an LSTM regresses the sequence-wide return from the whole state-action sequence; "contribution analysis is performed with difference of return predictions", i.e. the redistributed reward at step t is the change in the LSTM's return prediction; the goal is a return-equivalent decision process with "zero expected future rewards", so that "Q-value estimation simplifies to computing the mean of the immediate reward" — [RUDDER](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.07857)
- Theory: "For delayed rewards, TD requires exponentially many updates to correct the bias, where the number of updates is exponential in the number of delay steps. For MC learning the number of states affected by a delayed reward can grow exponentially with the number of delay steps" (Theorems A8, A10); optimal redistributions keep optimal policies (Theorem 1) and the optimal redistributed reward is the difference of Q-values (Eq. A65) — [RUDDER](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.07857)
- Requirements: "We assume γ = 1 and a finite horizon or an absorbing state"; three phases: safe exploration, a "lessons replay buffer" of episodes with unseen delayed rewards, LSTM training; "Human Expert Episodes ... can serve to fill the lessons replay buffer" and "Return decomposition will reward human key actions even for episodes with low return" — [RUDDER](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.07857)
- Artificial tasks (tabular, 100 trials): grid-world with delay 10/15/20, episodes to solve: RUDDER 3,520/3,062/3,814; MC 10,921/17,103/22,911; Q 66,141/115,352/171,572; at delay 50 MC needs 73,243 while RUDDER stays near 3,850 (Table A1); "RUDDER is exponentially faster with increasing delay than Q(λ)"; on Trace-Back it beats potential-based shaping, look-ahead and look-back advice "with p-values < 10^-17" — [RUDDER](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.07857)
- Atari (PPO baseline, 200M frames, 3 seeds x 10 trials, Table 1): Bowling 192 vs 56 (delay 200 frames), Solaris 1,827 vs 616, Venture 1,350 vs 820, Seaquest 4,770 vs 1,616; across 52 games RUDDER learns faster on 32 and has higher final average on 29; LSTM and redistribution are "restricted to sequence chunks of 500 frames" — [RUDDER](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.07857)
- Stated limitations: "RUDDER might not be effective when the reward is not delayed since LSTM learning takes extra time and has problems with very long sequences. Furthermore, reward redistribution may introduce disturbing spurious reward signals." — [RUDDER](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.07857)
- Two-player: none; single-agent Atari and gridworlds only — [RUDDER](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.07857)

**Align-RUDDER (Patil et al., ICML 2022).** See Key question 2 for the mechanism and demonstration counts. Requirements: episodic reward, demonstrations that "follow the same underlying strategy", a small event alphabet; tested on FourRooms/EightRooms gridworlds and Minecraft ObtainDiamond, single-agent — [Align-RUDDER](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.14108)

**Hindsight Credit Assignment (Harutyunyan et al., NeurIPS 2019).**
- Mechanism: learn a hindsight distribution h_k(a|x, y) = P(A_0 = a | X_k = y) (state-conditional) or h_z(a|x, z) = P(A_0 = a | Z = z) (return-conditional); Theorem 1 rewrites Q^π(x,a) = r(x,a) + E[Σ_k γ^k (h_k(a|x,X_k)/π(a|x)) R_k], so "each of the rewards R_k along the way is weighted by the ratio ... which exactly quantifies how relevant a was in achieving the corresponding state"; the ratio "is 1 if a is irrelevant"; the return-conditional advantage is A_z(X_s,A_s) = (1 − π(A_s|X_s)/h_z(A_s|X_s,Z_s)) Z_s, which needs no value function ("if h_z is estimated well, using complete rollouts is feasible without variance issues") — [HCA](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.02503)
- Cost: one extra classifier trained by cross-entropy to predict past actions from future states or returns, "learned in tandem with the control policy" — [HCA](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.02503)
- Results: tabular tasks only (Shortcut chain, Delayed Effect with aliased chain, Ambiguous Bandit), 100 runs, γ = 1; state-HCA "consistently able to maintain larger (and more accurate) advantages" on Shortcut and is "the most robust" to added noise on Delayed Effect; return-HCA "continues to improve on PG" when the state identity is hidden; no numeric scores given, curves only — [HCA](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.02503)
- Scaling: "Investigating the scalability of these algorithms in the deep reinforcement learning setting is an exciting problem for future research." No two-player test — [HCA](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.02503)

**Counterfactual Credit Assignment (Mesnard et al., ICML 2021).**
- Mechanism: extract a hindsight statistic Φ_t from the trajectory, learn V(X_t, Φ_t), and use it as the baseline; Theorem 1 (FC-PG) gives an unbiased estimator with an importance correction π(A_t|X_t)/P(A_t|X_t,Φ_t); Theorem 2 (CCA-PG): "If A_t is independent from Φ_t given X_t" the plain estimator Σ γ^t S_t (G_t − V(X_t,Φ_t)) is unbiased and "the hindsight advantage estimate has no higher variance than the forward one"; independence is enforced by "ensuring an action classifier does not gain any information about A_t when given access to Φ_t" (domain-adversarial style loss L_IM) — [CCA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.09464)
- Valid baseline inputs the paper names explicitly: "in multi-agent environments, actions of other agents at the same time step (Foerster et al., 2018) can be included in the baseline; and so can the full state (at time t) of the simulator when learning control from pixels, or the opponent's private observations (at time t) in Starcraft" — [CCA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.09464)
- Results: contextual bandit with exogenous reward noise (N = 10, reward −(C−A)^2 + ε_r): "increasing the variance of the exogenous noise leads to dramatic decrease of performance for the vanilla PG estimator ... the CCA-PG estimator is generally unaffected"; but "For very low level of exogenous noise however, CCA-PG suffers from a decrease in performance" because Φ is "not perfectly independent from A, leading to bias"; High-Variance Key-to-Door (apple reward randomly 1 or 10 per episode): "vanilla actor-critic ... needs around 3·10^9 environment steps to have an 80% probability of opening the door" while CCA-PG solves it "quickly and consistently" and "outperforms State- and Return-Conditional HCA"; medians over 10 seeds; only the single-action version is run — [CCA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.09464)
- Two-player: none tested; the StarCraft mention above is an example of a valid baseline input, not an experiment — [CCA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.09464)

**Temporal Value Transport (Hung et al., Nature Communications 2019).**
- Mechanism: a Reconstructive Memory Agent with external memory and content-based reads; when a read at a later step retrieves a memory, a "splice event" transports the later value estimate back as a bootstrap target to the read-from step, so "TVT still learned to acquire the key in P1 because it read from memory to predict the value function" — [TVT arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.06721)
- Task structure: three phases, no-reward action phase P1, a distractor phase P2 with incidental rewards (e.g. 30 s of apple collecting), and an exploitation phase P3; episodes of roughly 600 steps (reads "on time step 526 in P3" retrieve P1 memories); TVT also solved a 60-second distractor and a five-phase Key-to-Door-to-Match task; learning curves run to 2.5·10^9 agent steps — [TVT arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.06721)
- Why the distractor hurts: the paper defines a signal-to-noise ratio of the policy-gradient in P1 and notes "The standard solution is to average over larger data batches, which, with independent samples, linearly increases SNR ... at the expense of data efficiency" — [TVT arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.06721)
- Cost/limitation: needs the memory architecture; "When a task has no actual need for long-term temporal credit assignment, spurious triggering of splice events can send value back to earlier time points and bias the agent's activity." Single-agent only — [TVT arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.06721)

**Synthetic Returns (Raposo et al., 2021).**
- Mechanism: a network predicts the current reward as r_t ≈ g(s_t) Σ_{k<t} c(s_k) + b(s_t) with a sigmoid gate g; c(s_k) is then added to the reward as a "synthetic return"; the gate encodes an explicit assumption that "there is a single future state for which the contributions of past states is non-zero" — [Synthetic Returns](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.12425)
- Results: Chain task (17 states, 10 steps) solved where IMPALA fails; Key-to-Door (15-step key phase); Atari Skiing reaches human level and "solves the task using 25 times fewer environment steps than the previously published state-of-the-art set by Agent57"; single-agent, IMPALA-based — [Synthetic Returns](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.12425)

**Learning from demonstrations for sparse rewards.**
- DQfD (Hester et al., AAAI 2018): pre-trains on demonstrations with 1-step and n-step TD losses, a large-margin supervised classification loss and L2, then continues with prioritized replay that never overwrites demonstration data; demonstrations per game "range from 5,574 to 75,472 transitions" from a human playing "between three and twelve times"; "out-performs ... PDD DQN in 41 of 42 games on the first million steps, and on average it takes 83 million steps for PDD DQN to catch up"; beats the best demonstration in 14 of 42 games; state of the art in 11 games, e.g. Montezuma's Revenge 4,739.6 vs 3,705.5 (DQN+CTS), Pitfall 50.8 vs 0.0, Private Eye 40,908.2 vs 15,806.5; "pure imitation learning is worse than the demonstrator's performance in every game"; single-agent, discrete actions — [DQfD](https://arxiv.org/abs/1704.03732)
- Self-Imitation Learning (Oh et al., ICML 2018): an off-policy actor-critic term that imitates the agent's own past (s,a) pairs "only when the return in the past episode is greater than the agent's value estimate", derived as lower-bound soft Q-learning; Montezuma's Revenge 2,500 vs A3C+CTS 273, Reactor-PixelCNN 100, SimHash 75; Hero 33,069; Private Eye 8,684; Gravitar 2,722; Frostbite 6,439; Venture 0; on MuJoCo variants where "the modified tasks give an accumulated reward after every 20 steps ... the gap between PPO+SIL and PPO is larger on delayed-reward tasks"; the authors note exploitation "can also hurt performance in problems where there are proximal distractor rewards" — [SIL](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.05635)
- R2D3 (Gulcehre et al., 2019): 100 demonstrations per task, replay from separate agent and demo buffers with a "demo ratio" ρ; "the optimal demo ratio is very small (but non-zero)"; on the Hard-Eight suite "none of the baselines succeed in any of the eight environments" while R2D3 "learns six out of the eight tasks"; BC and DQfD "fail to learn a single task from the suite"; 256 actors, "at least 10 billion actor steps" — [R2D3](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.01387)
- Backward curriculum from one demonstration with a resettable simulator (Salimans & Chen, 2018): every episode "starts from a state in the demonstration"; reset points move earlier once "the proportion of rollouts that beat or at least tie the score of the demonstrator" exceeds a threshold; in the blind-cliff-walk analysis standard RL "scales exponentially in the number of states between rewards. Our method reduces this to quadratic scaling"; Montezuma's Revenge 74,500 (prior best 41,098), but "10,000 ... on average" under sticky actions; it "works well for Montezuma's Revenge, but much less well for some other Atari games we tried, like Gravitar and Pitfall"; the authors claim it "could offer benefits in multi-agent settings where we want to optimize performance against other opponents than the ones seen in the demonstration" (untested) — [Salimans & Chen](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.03381)

**AlphaStar-style human-data anchors (two-player, StarCraft II).**
- AlphaStar (Vinyals et al., Nature 2019): agents are "initialised to the parameters of the supervised learning agent" and "continually minimise the KL divergence between the supervised and current policy"; pseudo-rewards "measure the edit distance between sampled and executed build orders, and the Hamming distance between sampled and executed cumulative statistics", each "active ... with probability 25%" with "separate value functions and losses"; "We found our use of human data to be critical in achieving good performance with reinforcement learning (Fig. 3E)"; the supervised agent alone rated 3,699 MMR ("above 84% of human players"), the final agent 6,275/6,048/5,835 for Protoss/Terran/Zerg ("above 99.8%"); value learning uses TD(λ) with the opponent's observations as extra critic input "to decrease variance of the value estimates" (the figure labels in the unformatted PDF read 22% vs 82% average win-rate without/with opponent info, Fig. 3K); the stated limitation is that "imitation learning in AlphaStar requires a large amount of human demonstrations" — [AlphaStar Nature PDF](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf), [Nature page](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-019-1724-z)
- AlphaStar Unplugged (Mathieu et al., 2023): offline RL on human replays, versions 4.8.2–4.9.2 leave "nearly 5 million games"; filtering to MMR > 3500 ("the top 22% of players") leaves 2,776,466 episodes; each episode carries "the outcome, which can be 1 for a victory, 0 for a draw and −1 for a defeat, as well as the MMR of each player"; behavior cloning wins 84% vs the very_hard bot, BC fine-tuned on 21,836 winning episodes above 6,200 MMR wins 89%, offline actor-critic 93% (98% at temperature 0.8); "Q-function based approaches ... and algorithms that perform off-policy evaluation during learning ... sometimes fail to win a single game against our weakest opponent, and all fail to outperform our unconditional behavior cloning baseline"; the successful recipe is "first train a model to estimate the behavior policy and behavior value function. Then, use the behavior value function to improve the policy"; training BC on only 10% of the episodes "is close to the best" — [AlphaStar Unplugged](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.03526)
- OpenAI Five (Berner et al., 2019; two-team Dota 2): "In order to simplify the credit assignment problem ... we use a more detailed reward function. Our shaped reward is modeled loosely after potential-based shaping functions, though the guarantees therein do not apply here"; team spirit annealed 0.3 → 0.8 (planned 1.0), GAE horizon 180 s → 360 s (planned 840 s), GAE λ = 0.95, roughly 20,000 steps per episode; the authors note the agent's playstyle "is likely influenced by our shaped reward function" — [OpenAI Five](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.06680)

**Hindsight relabelling in competitive settings.**
- Competitive Experience Replay (Liu et al., ICLR 2019) is the only "competitive" hindsight-relabelling method found; it trains "a pair of agents on the same task" and "asymmetrically augments these sparse rewards", relabelling "based on intra-agent behavior" whereas HER relabels per agent; the "interact-CER" variant, where agent B starts "from a random off-policy sample of π_A", is "more restricted to re-settable environments"; tasks are single-task ant mazes and robot-arm manipulation with binary rewards, trained with MADDPG; results are success-rate curves, with CER+HER best. This is an exploration curriculum in a single-agent task, not credit assignment in an adversarial game — [CER](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.00528)

**Potential-based and learned shaping.**
- Ng, Harada & Russell (ICML 1999) proved that adding F(s,a,s') = γΦ(s') − Φ(s) preserves the optimal policy; Wiewiora (JAIR 2003) proved "a reinforcement learner with initial Q-values based on the shaping algorithm's potential function make the same updates throughout learning as a learner receiving potential-based shaping rewards" — [Wiewiora 2003](https://arxiv.org/abs/1106.5267), [Ng et al. 1999 PDF](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~pabbeel/cs287-fa09/readings/NgHaradaRussell-shaping-ICML1999.pdf)
- Shaping from demonstrations (Brys et al., IJCAI 2015): potential Φ_D(s,a) = max over demonstrated pairs with the same action of a Gaussian similarity; used either as F_D(s,a,s',a') = γΦ_D(s',a') − Φ_D(s,a) or as Q-initialization; tested on Cart Pole and Mario; claims "significantly fewer demonstrations", robustness to suboptimal demonstrations and "much faster learning than ... HAT" (curves only) — [Brys et al. 2015](https://www.ijcai.org/Proceedings/15/Papers/472.pdf)
- RUDDER's own comparison: on Trace-Back, potential-based shaping with look-ahead/look-back advice was beaten "exponentially" by return decomposition when a distractor immediate reward (−50 for the optimal action at t = 2) is present — [RUDDER](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.07857)

**Group baselines (RLOO, GRPO).** See Key question 3. Neither was tested on a game; both are LLM single-turn settings — [RLOO/Ahmadian 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14740), [DeepSeekMath/GRPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03300)

**Methods that exploit an exact, resettable simulator.**
- TRPO "vine" (Schulman et al., ICML 2015): choose a "rollout set" of N states along trunk trajectories, sample K actions per state, estimate Q̂(s_n, a_{n,k}) "by performing a rollout (i.e., a short trajectory)"; "We can greatly reduce the variance of the Q-value differences between rollouts by using the same random number sequence for the noise in each of the K rollouts, i.e., common random numbers"; "In small, finite action spaces, we can generate a rollout for every possible action"; the self-normalized estimator "removes the need to use a baseline"; "our local estimate of the objective has much lower variance given the same number of Q-value samples"; the method "requires the system to be restored to particular states, which is typically only possible in simulation" — [TRPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.05477)
- Common random numbers for rollout planning (Yadav et al., RLJ 2026): proposes "a new estimator of the value difference between policies that are identical beyond some time step" and "a simple recipe for (provably) reducing variance in relative utility when simulations invoke a rollout policy beyond some depth", with a UCT deployment on the two-player dice game Ludo; the primary reference for CRN in MDPs is PEGASUS (Ng & Jordan 2000), which treats "a stochastic MDP ... as a distribution of deterministic MDPs" — [CRN rollouts](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.04732)
- Many-actions policy gradient (Nauman & Cygan, ICML 2023): sampling N actions per state (via environment rewinding or a Q-model) is unbiased and "yields lower or equal variance as compared to single-action SPG with equal trajectory length"; the "many-actions optimality condition" says it beats a proportionally longer trajectory when the action-dependent covariance dominates; for bandits it is "never optimal ... if δ ≥ 1 (ie. the cost of acquiring an additional action sample is equal to or greater than the cost of acquiring an additional state sample)"; measured on DeepMind Control, the policy-dependent share of gradient variance was 61%–99% (Table 1), yet (T,N) = (1024,2) beat (2048,1) only on some tasks (Cart Swingup 837 vs 801, Finger Spin 304 vs 281) and lost on others (Reacher Easy 428 vs 776) — [Many-actions PG](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v202/nauman23a.html)
- Expert Iteration (Anthony, Tian & Barber, NeurIPS 2017; two-player Hex 9x9): the expert is MCTS "with 10,000 iterations" per move guided by the apprentice; policy targets are the search's move choice and the loop runs on batches of 243,000 expert moves; "compared to REINFORCE, EXIT learns stronger policies faster. EXIT also shows no sign of instability" from the same warm-started network (5 runs, Elo curves in Fig. 2, no scalar gap stated); the final agent "won 75.3% of games against 10,000 iteration-MOHEX ... 59.3% against 100,000 iteration-MOHEX, and 55.6% against 4 second per move-MOHEX" — [ExIt](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08439); the follow-up Policy Gradient Search replaces the tree with online policy-gradient updates of the simulation policy and "was able [to] defeat MoHex 2.0" in 9x9 Hex — [PGS](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.03646)
- KataGo (Wu, 2019/2020; two-player Go): "In the AlphaZero process, the game outcome value target is highly data-limited, with only one noisy binary result per entire game"; playout cap randomization (a full search on a small fraction of turns, fast searches elsewhere, only full-search turns recorded) relieves the policy/value data tension; "the game outcome is noisy and binary, [but] it is a direct function of finer variables: the final score difference and the ownership of each board location" so auxiliary ownership and score heads are added and "noticeably improve the efficiency of learning ... even up through the ends of those runs"; overall "a factor of 50 reduction" in compute versus ELF OpenGo (1.4 GPU-years vs ~74) — [KataGo](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10565)
- GiGPO (Feng et al., NeurIPS 2025): group-based advantages at two levels: episode-level GRPO over N = 8 trajectories from identical initial states, plus "anchor state grouping" that "retroactively identifies the repeated environment states ... across trajectories" to form step-level groups; gains of "> 12% on ALFWorld and > 9% on WebShop over GRPO" with "identical LLM rollout" and "< 0.002% time cost"; in ALFWorld "the majority of states (over 65%) recur across trajectories" — [GiGPO](https://personal.ntu.edu.sg/boan/papers/NeurIPS25_GiGPO.pdf)
- CRAFT (Meng & Chen, 2026): reuses "the G − 1 sibling rollouts that GRPO already samples" to form "a signed per-token credit at essentially no extra compute", with an O(1/G) variance bound; agentic-LLM benchmarks (ALFWorld, WebShop, Search-QA), no game — [CRAFT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.29476)

**Per-unit counterfactual baselines in multi-unit games.**
- COMA (Foerster et al., AAAI 2018): centralised critic Q(s, u); advantage for agent a uses "a counterfactual baseline that marginalises out a single agent's action, while keeping the other agents' actions fixed", computed "in a single forward pass" for all of one agent's actions; StarCraft micromanagement with a restricted field of view against the built-in AI, mean win % over the final 1,000 evaluation episodes (Table 1): 3m COMA 87 vs central-QV 83, central-V 83, IAC-Q 56, IAC-V 47; 5m 81 vs 71/67/58/63; 5w 82 vs 76/65/57/18; 2d_3z 47 vs 39/36/19/27 (heuristic 63); 35 trials; TD(λ) with λ = 0.8; cooperative common reward ("sum of damage inflicted"), not self-play — [COMA](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08926)
- MACA (Zhao & Xie, AISTATS 2025): k-level counterfactual advantages that marginalise "the k agents' actions by their policies while keeping other agents' actions fixed"; on SMACv2 5_vs_5 maps MACA 79.0/74.4/63.4 (protoss/terran/zerg) vs MAPPO 56.5/50.5/42.3 and COMA 2.0/7.0/4.5; on SMAC 10m_vs_11m 100.0 vs MAPPO 87.0 — [MACA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.06836)

### Inferences
- The only credit-assignment methods with two-player evidence are the search-target family (ExIt, KataGo) and the human-anchored actor-critics (AlphaStar); return decomposition and hindsight baselines have no adversarial validation at all, so their transfer to Wesnoth is speculative on the method's own terms.
- ExIt's expert used 10,000 MCTS iterations per move on a 9x9 board; the project's own negative result used 32 evaluations over ~350 atomic actions, so the ExIt evidence does not contradict the project's finding that a 32-evaluation search is not a teacher. Speculative transfer: turn-level search (fewer, larger decisions) is the direction ExIt's budget suggests.
- CCA's explicit list of valid baseline inputs (opponent's private observations, other agents' simultaneous actions, the full simulator state at time t) is directly available from a god-view simulator: a critic conditioned on the opponent's hidden units and on this turn's realised combat luck is a valid, lower-variance baseline as long as the conditioning information is independent of the agent's action given the state. AlphaStar already did the opponent-observation part. Speculative transfer: with a pre-committed per-game luck stream (common random numbers), the stream itself is action-independent and can be conditioned on; the realised dice outcomes are not, because which attacks happen depends on the action.
- RUDDER's redistributed reward is the step-to-step difference of a sequence-wide return regression; on the human corpus this coincides with potential-based shaping using a hindsight (bidirectional) value model, which by Ng et al. is policy-invariant and by Wiewiora equivalent to a Q initialisation. The project's value head already ranks positions well but cannot rank alternative turns; RUDDER-style decomposition credits only the taken action along the realised trajectory (HCA's "no counterfactuals" issue 4), so it would not by itself supply the within-state contrast that is missing.
- OpenAI Five and AlphaStar both solved the credit problem in long adversarial games by making the reward dense (shaped rewards, pseudo-rewards for human-like statistics), not by decomposition; both authors note the shaping changes the learned style.

### Gaps
- No two-player or self-play experiment exists for RUDDER, Align-RUDDER, HCA, CCA, TVT or Synthetic Returns; their behaviour under a non-stationary opponent is unmeasured.
- TRPO reports vine's variance advantage qualitatively; the vine-vs-single-path score tables were not extracted, so the magnitude is not recorded here.
- ExIt's REINFORCE comparison is given as Elo curves (Figure 2) without a stated Elo gap.
- Brys et al. (2015) results are curves; no scalar speed-up was extracted.
- Kool et al. 2019 ("Buy 4 REINFORCE Samples, Get a Baseline for Free!") could not be fetched (OpenReview bot check); its content is cited through Ahmadian et al. 2024 and Kool et al. 2020.

## Key question 2: Align-RUDDER — how few demonstrations, how is per-step redistribution derived, and would 17,000 human games suffice?

### Takeaway
Align-RUDDER needs as few as 2–10 demonstrations because it replaces the LSTM with a bioinformatics profile model over a small alphabet of "events", and it assumes the demonstrations share one strategy; it reached a diamond in Minecraft in only 0.1% of runs from 10 demonstrations. With 17,000 games the data-starvation argument that motivates alignment disappears, and the original LSTM RUDDER (which the RUDDER paper itself says can be fed human expert episodes) is the better-matched variant; the alignment variant's assumptions (about 20 events, one shared strategy) do not fit a 21-map, multi-faction corpus.

### Cited Findings
- Motivation: "RUDDER's LSTM ... as a deep learning method requires many examples for learning. Therefore, we introduce Align-RUDDER, which replaces RUDDER's LSTM with a profile model obtained from multiple sequence alignment (MSA) of the demonstrations"; "sequence alignment requires only two examples to generalize well as known from bioinformatics" — [Align-RUDDER](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.14108)
- The five steps: "(I) Define events to turn episodes of state-action sequences into sequences of events. (II) Determine an alignment scoring scheme ... (III) Perform a multiple sequence alignment (MSA) of the demonstrations. (IV) Compute the profile model like a PSSM. (V) Redistribute the reward: Each subsequence τ_t of a new episode τ is aligned to the profile. The redistributed reward R_{t+1} is proportional to the difference of scores S ... R_{t+1} ∝ S(τ_t) − S(τ_{t−1})" — [Align-RUDDER](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.14108)
- Events: "we consider differences of consecutive states to detect a change caused by an important event ... An event is defined as a cluster of state differences. We use similarity-based clustering like affinity propagation"; "Alignment techniques assume that sequences consist of few symbols, e.g. about 20 symbols, the events. It is crucial to keep the number of events small ... If there are many events, then two demonstrations might have few events that can be matched, which cannot be well distinguished from random alignments" — [Align-RUDDER](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.14108)
- Strategy assumption: "For the alignment, we assume that the demonstrations follow the same underlying strategy, therefore they are similar to each other analog to being evolutionary related"; ClustalW's guide tree "allows to identify multiple strategies" — [Align-RUDDER](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.14108)
- Gridworld results (FourRooms, EightRooms 12x24, stochastic transitions, T = 200, reward only at the end, demonstrations from an optimal policy with 0.2 exploration, 100 trials): demonstration counts {2, 5, 10, 50, 100}; "Align-RUDDER significantly outperforms all other methods, for ≤ 10 demonstrations (p-values < 10^-10)" on Task I and "(p-values < 10^-19)" on Task II, against BC+Q-learning, SQIL, DQfD and LSTM-RUDDER; GAIL "failed to solve the two artificial tasks"; ablations show results "stay similar" down to 8 clusters and degrade at 2 or 5 clusters; transition stochasticity of 1% and 5% was tested — [Align-RUDDER](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.14108)
- Minecraft ObtainDiamond: only the diamond gives reward; the MineRL data "contains demonstrations which are insufficient to directly learn a single policy (117 demonstrations, 67 mined a diamond)"; 19 event clusters "characterized by inventory changes"; "Using only ten demonstrations, Align-RUDDER is able to learn to mine a diamond. A diamond is obtained in 0.1% of the cases" under the 8-million-timestep limit, with 31 extracted sub-tasks each executed by a sub-agent under a manager; a heuristic competitor (ForgER++) reached 0.0667% without a timestep limit — [Align-RUDDER](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.14108)
- Learning recipe: redistributed rewards feed either RUDDER's Q-estimation, PPO (used for Minecraft), or Q-learning; "Recently, the convergence of RUDDER learning methods has been proven under commonly used assumptions" — [Align-RUDDER](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.14108)
- The LSTM alternative on human data: "Human Expert Episodes ... can serve to fill the lessons replay buffer. Learning can be sped up considerably when LSTM identifies human key actions. Return decomposition will reward human key actions even for episodes with low return since other actions that thwart high returns receive negative reward" — [RUDDER](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.07857)
- What RUDDER's LSTM regression needs: sequence-wide return targets from complete episodes; on Atari it was "restricted to sequence chunks of 500 frames" and the authors warn the LSTM "has problems with very long sequences" — [RUDDER](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.07857)

### Inferences
- 17,000 games is three orders of magnitude beyond Align-RUDDER's regime (2–10 demonstrations) and comparable to what a sequence model needs; the alignment variant buys nothing here and its event-alphabet constraint (about 20 symbols) would force an aggressive abstraction of Wesnoth turns (e.g. "first village taken", "leader exposed") that discards the unit-level detail the model must credit. Speculative transfer: alignment could still serve as a strategy-extraction tool per map/faction pair, but not as the redistribution model.
- LSTM/transformer RUDDER on the corpus is feasible in principle (one return-regression target per game, a few hundred decisions per side), and the 500-frame chunking used for Atari indicates the authors did not run it over thousands of steps. The resulting per-decision credit is a hindsight regression difference along the played trajectory, not a counterfactual against alternative moves.
- The 0.1% Minecraft success rate, on a task with a hierarchical 31-sub-task structure and only 10 demonstrations, is the only "complex task" number for the method; it does not establish that the redistribution produces a strong policy, only that it produces a learnable sub-goal sequence.
- Player ratings in the corpus are not used by either RUDDER variant; AlphaStar Unplugged's MMR-conditioning of behavior cloning ("similar to Return-Conditioned Behavior Cloning with the MMR as the reward") is the closest precedent for exploiting them, and that paper's return-conditioned baselines did not beat plain BC.

### Gaps
- No source applies RUDDER or Align-RUDDER to a corpus of thousands of games, to a two-player game, or to sequences of several hundred decisions; the "very long sequences" caveat is unquantified.
- Whether redistributed rewards trained from human games transfer to self-play (where the state distribution shifts) is untested in any source.

## Key question 3: Group-baseline policy gradients (RLOO, GRPO, precedents) — how many samples per state, and how does the per-state baseline behave when outcome noise dominates (win probability near 0.5)?

### Takeaway
RLOO used k = 2 or 4 samples per prompt and GRPO used 64 outputs per question; both treat the whole trajectory as one action with a single terminal reward, and their gain over PPO was measured on LLM reward models, not on games. With binary outcomes near p = 0.5 the group baseline is an average of Bernoulli draws whose standard error is 0.5/sqrt(k−1), and GRPO's std-normalisation rescales every group's advantages to unit size whether the true effect is large or nil; the many-actions theory says extra samples from the same state only pay when action-dependent variance dominates the total.

### Cited Findings
- RLOO estimator: (1/k) Σ_i [R(y^(i),x) − (1/(k−1)) Σ_{j≠i} R(y^(j),x)] ∇log π(y^(i)|x) for k i.i.d. samples per prompt; "The rewards for each sample can serve all other samples as a baseline" and the update averages the k gradient estimates — [RLOO/Ahmadian 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14740)
- Sample counts and results: experiments use "k = {2, 4}"; final win-rates (Table 1): TL;DR RLOO k=4 77.9, k=2 74.2, REINFORCE with moving-average baseline 70.7, Vanilla PG 70.4, PPO 67.6, DPO 66.6; HH (Llama) RLOO k=4 64.1, PPO 32.0; "RLOO with k = 4 achieves the highest win-rates, outperforming PPO by 10.3, 14.5, and 32.1"; "RLOO_k=2 either closely matches or outperforms RAFT_k=4" — [RLOO/Ahmadian 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14740)
- Whole-trajectory-as-one-action argument: "in LLM RLHF a r(x, y) is only attributed to the <EOS> token ... the environment dynamics are fully deterministic ... Hence, the problem can be reduced to a bandit problem"; "modeling partial sequences is unnecessary in the context of RLHF" — [RLOO/Ahmadian 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14740)
- Noise claim: "RLOO is relatively robust to noise and KL penalty sensitivity" compared with RAFT, shown by injecting reward noise and varying β ∈ {0.25, 0.5, 1}; the comparison is against RAFT (which keeps only the top-ranked sample), not against a learned critic — [RLOO/Ahmadian 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14740)
- Precedent: Kool, van Hoof & Welling derive "a policy gradient estimator ... [with] a built-in control variate which is obtained without additional model evaluations", including the sampling-without-replacement version where the baseline uses "second order leave-one-out ratio[s]" to stay unbiased with dependent samples; tested on a Bernoulli toy problem, a categorical VAE and structured prediction — [Kool et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.06043); the 2019 workshop paper "Buy 4 REINFORCE Samples, Get a Baseline for Free!" is the origin cited by Ahmadian et al. — [RLOO/Ahmadian 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14740)
- GRPO: "for each question q, GRPO samples a group of outputs {o_1, ..., o_G} from the old policy" and "these rewards are normalized by subtracting the group average and dividing by the group standard deviation. Outcome supervision provides the normalized reward at the end of each output o_i and sets the advantages Â_{i,t} of all tokens in the output as the normalized reward"; hyperparameters: "For each question, we sample 64 outputs", policy learning rate 1e-6, KL coefficient 0.04, batch 1024, "a single update following each exploration stage"; results GSM8K 82.9% → 88.2%, MATH 46.8% → 51.7% — [DeepSeekMath/GRPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03300)
- What GRPO changed: "RL enhances Maj@K's performance but not Pass@K ... it seems that the improvement is attributed to boosting the correct response from TopK rather than the enhancement of fundamental capabilities" — [DeepSeekMath/GRPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03300)
- When extra per-state samples pay: the many-actions estimator "yields lower or equal variance as compared to single-action SPG with equal trajectory length"; it is preferable to lengthening trajectories only when "the weighted sum of MDP covariances exceeds the variance of the Markov Chain underlying the MDP", i.e. "in problems where action-dependent covariance constitutes a sizeable portion of the total SPG variance"; for bandits with per-sample cost δ ≥ 1 it "is never optimal" — [Many-actions PG](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v202/nauman23a.html)
- Vine's per-state groups: K actions per rollout-set state with common random numbers; "In small, finite action spaces, we can generate a rollout for every possible action from a given state" — [TRPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.05477)
- Step-level groups without extra rollouts: GiGPO groups actions "stemming from the same state" when states recur across the N = 8 trajectories; over 65% of ALFWorld states recurred — [GiGPO](https://personal.ntu.edu.sg/boan/papers/NeurIPS25_GiGPO.pdf)
- The information ceiling per binary sample: the information a binary outcome can carry is the binary entropy "−p log(p) − (1−p) log(1−p) bits/sample", maximal at p = 0.5; supervised targets carry "−log(p) bits/sample" — [Dwarkesh Patel, Nov 2025](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/bits-per-sample)
- Learned helplessness under exogenous noise: with a return-predictive hindsight baseline that is not action-independent, "the agent will assume the outcome is entirely controlled by chance, and that all actions are equivalent, resulting in a form of learned helplessness"; this is why CCA enforces independence — [CCA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.09464)

### Inferences
- For a binary outcome with win probability p, the leave-one-out baseline from k samples has standard error sqrt(p(1−p)/(k−1)) ≈ 0.5/sqrt(k−1) at p = 0.5, and the advantage of one sample is bounded by 1 in magnitude; detecting a per-decision win-probability effect of size δ needs on the order of p(1−p)/δ^2 paired samples per state (about 100 for δ = 0.05, the project's measured mean best-turn gain). The RLOO/GRPO papers never face this regime because their reward models are continuous and their prompts have many distinguishable completions.
- GRPO's division by the group standard deviation turns any group with mixed outcomes into ±O(1) advantages regardless of how small the true effect is, and yields zero advantage (division by zero guarded in implementations) for all-win or all-loss groups; at p ≈ 0.5 the normalisation therefore amplifies pure luck. RLOO's unnormalised form keeps the magnitude honest but not the sign. This is a property of the formulas, not a reported finding.
- The many-actions condition is the relevant test for "sibling rollouts per state": it pays only if a large share of return variance is attributable to the action at that state rather than to later luck and later decisions. In a 300-decision game with combat dice, most variance at one atomic decision is downstream, so per-state groups at the atomic level are predicted to lose to more games; per-turn groups with short common-random-number rollouts truncated by the value head (the CRN-rollouts "identical beyond some depth" estimator) are the version of the idea that the theory favours. Speculative transfer.
- GiGPO's anchor-state trick needs recurring states, which a wargame with hundreds of units will not produce naturally; the forkable simulator can manufacture the recurrence (vine), at the cost of extra rollouts that GiGPO avoids.

### Gaps
- No source reports RLOO or GRPO behaviour with binary rewards at success rates near 0.5 on long-horizon tasks; the LLM math results have per-question pass rates spread over [0,1] and group sizes of 64.
- No game-playing precedent for RLOO/GRPO was found beyond the LLM-agent benchmarks (ALFWorld, WebShop); the "game-playing precedents" of per-state multi-sample baselines are vine (MuJoCo/Atari, single-agent) and COMA (cooperative StarCraft micro).

## Key question 4: Are there methods that estimate the advantage of a whole turn or macro-action rather than an atomic action, and did they help credit assignment in multi-unit games?

### Takeaway
Two families exist: hierarchical critics that evaluate a whole turn/utterance and let a low-level policy fill it in (ArCHer, turn-level reward designs for LLM agents; the options framework in the survey), and per-unit counterfactual baselines that hold the rest of the turn fixed while marginalising one unit's action (COMA, MACA). Both report gains — ArCHer about 100x sample efficiency over PPO on multi-turn LLM tasks, COMA/MACA large win-rate gains on StarCraft micromanagement — but none was tested in a turn-based multi-unit game where one side moves many units per turn against a learning opponent.

### Cited Findings
- Survey position on temporally extended actions: "it is easier to assign credit to macro-actions since a sequence of options is usually shorter than a sequence of atomic actions, reducing the overall temporal distance to the time of achieving the goal"; the option-critic architecture "mirrors the actor-critic architecture but considering options rather than actions"; the survey explicitly excludes multi-agent credit assignment ("We also do not discuss CA in multi-agent RL") — [Pignatelli et al. survey](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.01072)
- ArCHer (Zhou et al., 2024): "a high-level off-policy RL algorithm that trains a value function to aggregate reward over utterances, and a low-level RL algorithm that utilizes this high-level value function (in place of a reward model used in single-turn RL) to train a token-by-token policy within each utterance or turn"; the high-level critic "is trained at a coarser time-scale"; "algorithms derived from ArCHer are 100x more sample efficient than on-policy methods such as PPO" on multi-turn language-agent tasks — [ArCHer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.19446)
- Turn-level reward design (Wei et al., 2026 preprint): categorises reward structures as terminal, delayed, and per-turn, derives GRPO and PPO variants for each, and finds "dense per-turn reward structures consistently outperform sparse terminal and delayed reward structures" on multi-turn search and game-agent tasks; per-turn rewards are "assigned to the final token of each turn" — [Wei et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.11821)
- COMA's per-unit advantage within a joint action: "COMA computes a separate baseline for each agent that relies on the centralised critic to reason about counterfactuals in which only that agent's action changes", avoiding "extra simulations, approximations, or assumptions regarding appropriate default actions"; win-rate gains over the same critic with a plain V baseline (central-QV) were 87 vs 83 (3m), 81 vs 71 (5m), 82 vs 76 (5w), 47 vs 39 (2d_3z) — [COMA](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08926)
- MACA extends the baseline to subsets of k agents ("marginalizes out the k agents' actions by their policies while keeping other agents' actions fixed") and reports COMA collapsing on modern SMAC/SMACv2 maps (COMA 0.5–7.0% vs MACA 63–100%) — [MACA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.06836)
- Macro-actions as atomic extra actions can hurt: "in many scenarios the naive addition of macro-actions does not lead to better exploration, but rather the opposite" because "one typically treats each potential macro-action as independent and atomic, hence strictly increasing the search space"; a meta-learned similarity penalty between actions and macro-actions gives "significant improvements over the RAINBOW-DQN baseline" on Atari and StreetFighter II (curves) — [Hosu et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.13690)
- Turn-scale credit in real-time strategy: OpenAI Five's GAE horizon (the time over which rewards are integrated) was raised from 180 s to 360 s during training, with 840 s planned; the authors use this horizon "as a proxy for the long-term credit assignment" ability — [OpenAI Five](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.06680)
- Whole-trajectory-as-one-action is the RLOO position (see Q3): with one terminal reward and deterministic dynamics "it is more appropriate and efficient to model the entire generation as a single action" — [RLOO/Ahmadian 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14740)
- Search over turns rather than atomic moves is the ExIt regime: the expert's target is one move per position with 10,000 simulations, and the apprentice imitates it — [ExIt](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08439)

### Inferences
- The ArCHer decomposition maps directly onto a turn-based wargame: a turn-level critic trained by TD across side-turns (about 30–60 boundaries per game instead of 300 decisions) and a within-turn policy trained against that critic. The reported 100x is on LLM agent tasks with a few turns; no source tests it with an adversarial opponent. Speculative transfer.
- COMA is the natural per-unit credit method for a multi-unit turn: the units' actions within one side-turn are a joint action, and a centralised (god-view) critic can score every alternative action of one unit in one forward pass. Two cautions from the sources: COMA's gains over a plain V baseline were 4–10 win-rate points on 3–5 unit maps, and later benchmarks report COMA near zero; the critic must be accurate enough to rank alternatives, which is exactly the capability the project's value head lacks at the turn level.
- The macro-action literature warns that adding turn-level macro-actions to the action set worsens exploration unless the shared structure between macro and atomic actions is exploited; a factorised turn policy (sequence of atomic actions) with a turn-level critic avoids enlarging the action set.

### Gaps
- No source estimates the advantage of an entire multi-unit turn against alternative turns in a turn-based game; COMA/MACA evaluate single-unit counterfactuals in simultaneous-move micro, ArCHer evaluates utterances in language tasks.
- No measured comparison between turn-level and atomic-level advantage estimation under a binary outcome.

## Key question 5: How much information does a binary outcome carry per game, and which methods provably or empirically extract more than plain Monte Carlo returns?

### Takeaway
A binary outcome carries at most one bit per game (exactly one at p = 0.5) about a trajectory of hundreds of decisions, and the quantity that matters for learning is the mutual information between the action at a state and the return, which is what makes "sparse" problems hard (Arumugam et al.). Methods that demonstrably beat plain Monte Carlo do so either by lowering variance without adding information (hindsight baselines with a proof of no-higher variance; common random numbers; return redistribution that changes bias/variance, not information), or by adding information from other sources: auxiliary targets that decompose the outcome (KataGo, multi-labelled value nets), demonstrations' actions (behaviour cloning dominates every offline-RL baseline on StarCraft II), and search.

### Cited Findings
- Information sparsity as the real obstacle: "it is not the sparsity of the reward itself that causes difficulty in credit assignment, but rather the information sparsity"; an MDP is "ε-information-sparse if sup_{π_0} I^{π_0}(A; Z | S) ≤ ε", and "a decision-making problem where the actions of the initial policy have little to no dependence on returns cannot acquire the signal needed to learn an optimal policy; sparse-reward problems are a natural example of this" — [Arumugam, Henderson & Bacon 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.06224)
- Bits per sample: for RL with a binary outcome the information per sample is bounded by "−p log(p) − (1−p) log(1−p) bits/sample" (the entropy of the outcome), against "−log(p) bits/sample" for a supervised label; RL's information density matches pretraining only late in training, and early training additionally "suffers from extreme gradient noise" (secondary essay, credits Toby Ord and Thinking Machines) — [Dwarkesh Patel, Nov 2025](https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/bits-per-sample); "RL accumulates 1/rollout_length bits per sample compared to pretraining" and works because the bits "are precisely targeted" and RL "can only bring out behaviours that are already in the base models" (secondary essay) — [Millidge, Jul 2026](https://www.beren.io/2026-07-26-How-Can-LLM-RL-Work-Despite-Information-Theoretic-Inefficiency/)
- One label per game in practice: AlphaGo's value network trained on complete KGS games "memorized the game outcomes rather than generalizing to new positions, achieving a minimum MSE of 0.37 on the test set, compared to 0.19 on the training set", because "successive positions are strongly correlated ... but the regression target is shared for the entire game"; the fix was "a new self-play data set consisting of 30 million distinct positions, each sampled from a separate game", giving MSEs of 0.226/0.234 — [AlphaGo Nature PDF](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/alphago/AlphaGoNaturePaper.pdf), [Nature page](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature16961)
- KataGo: "the game outcome value target is highly data-limited, with only one noisy binary result per entire game"; decomposing the outcome into ownership and score targets "noticeably improve[s] the efficiency of learning" even late in training; playout cap randomization exists to generate more games per compute for the value target — [KataGo](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10565)
- Multi-labelled value network (Wu et al., 2017): training values for many komi settings simultaneously plus a board-ownership head lowers MSE, and "the program based on the ML value network wins by a rate of 67.6% against the program based on the value network alone" — [Multi-labelled value networks](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.10701)
- AlphaZero's target is the raw outcome: "the terminal position s_T is scored ... to compute the game outcome z: −1 for a loss, 0 for a draw, and +1 for a win", and the value head is trained to E[z|s]; the authors note that binary outcomes "may help neural network training" relative to chess/shogi's structure — [AlphaZero](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.01815)
- Demonstrated actions carry more than outcomes: on 2.8M StarCraft II human games, behavior cloning reached 84% vs the very_hard bot while every Q-function-based and return-conditioned method "fail[ed] to outperform our unconditional behavior cloning baseline"; the only improvements came from one-step policy improvement on the behaviour value (93%, 98% at temperature 0.8) — [AlphaStar Unplugged](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.03526)
- Variance-reduction results with proofs: CCA's hindsight advantage "has no higher variance than the forward one" (Theorem 2) when the statistic is action-independent — [CCA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.09464); the many-actions estimator "is unbiased and yields lower or equal variance as compared to single-action SPG with equal trajectory length" — [Many-actions PG](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v202/nauman23a.html); common random numbers give "a simple recipe for (provably) reducing variance in relative utility when simulations invoke a rollout policy beyond some depth" — [CRN rollouts](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.04732); vine with CRN "has much lower variance given the same number of Q-value samples" — [TRPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.05477)
- Bias/variance rather than information: RUDDER's Theorems A8/A10 quantify TD's exponential bias correction time and MC's exponential growth in the number of high-variance estimates with delay; the redistribution is "return-equivalent" (same total return), i.e. it re-times the same label — [RUDDER](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.07857)
- HCA's return-conditional form uses "returns generated by any action A_0 to update the values of all actions, to the extent that they are relevant", i.e. it extracts counterfactual credit from one trajectory only through the learned hindsight classifier — [HCA](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.02503)
- Distractor variance in policy gradients: TVT frames the problem as SNR of the P1 gradient and notes averaging over batches "linearly increases SNR" only with independent samples — [TVT arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.06721)
- The survey's open question: "What is the minimum rate of successes G_min = |C*|/|C| that a CA method requires to start converging to an optimal policy?" is "a fundamental open question in the current literature" — [Pignatelli et al. survey](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.01072)
- Search as information: ExIt's tree search "considers multiple possible responses to the moves it recommends" so plans "don't overfit to a current opponent", and it beat REINFORCE from the same start — [ExIt](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08439)

### Inferences
- At p ≈ 0.5 each game yields one bit about the whole trajectory; 17,019 corpus games therefore hold at most ~17,000 bits of outcome information for a value head, versus AlphaGo's 30 million independent labels. Per Arumugam's definition, the atomic-decision problem is information-sparse (I(A; Z | S) per decision is tiny), which is consistent with the project's measured result that outcome-graded rollout contrasts carried too little information to fit a network.
- Redistribution (RUDDER, SR, TVT) and hindsight baselines (HCA, CCA) do not add bits; they change where the one bit lands and how noisy the estimate is. The approaches that add bits are auxiliary targets derived from the terminal state (KataGo: ownership and score; the wargame analogue is final and intermediate material, villages, gold, per-hex control, unit survival), demonstration actions (log|A| bits per decision), and exact search. Speculative transfer: the corpus records full trajectories, so KataGo-style auxiliary targets are available at zero extra data cost and are the cheapest lever the literature supports for the value head; whether they improve turn-ranking (as opposed to position ranking) is untested anywhere.
- Ratings on 17,000 games are an additional label per game that no credit-assignment method in this survey uses; AlphaStar Unplugged's MMR-conditioning is the only precedent and it was used for BC quality control, not credit.
- Common random numbers are the one variance lever the sources prove and that an exact simulator makes free; its benefit vanishes once paired trajectories diverge, so it favours short contrasts (one turn's combats resolved under the same dice) bootstrapped by a value estimate.

### Gaps
- No source quantifies bits-per-game or sample-complexity for learning a value or advantage function from binary game outcomes in a two-player game; the AlphaGo overfitting numbers are the closest empirical evidence.
- No source measures how much of the outcome variance in a strategy game is attributable to a single turn's decision versus later play, which is the quantity that decides whether per-state sibling rollouts pay (the many-actions condition).
- The information-theoretic essays are secondary sources (blogs) and were included only for the entropy bound they state; the primary results they credit (Toby Ord, Thinking Machines) were not fetched.
