# Sample-efficient search-based self-play for games with exact simulators, large or factored action spaces, and small compute budgets

Scope note. Primary sources were fetched where possible (arXiv/ar5iv full text, author-hosted PDFs, the first author's PhD thesis for the Gumbel paper because OpenReview is CAPTCHA-gated). Where only an abstract or a secondary write-up was reachable, the finding says so. Years are publication years. "Target" below means the caller's application: a two-player turn-based hex wargame with a bit-exact simulator, about 350 legal atomic actions per decision, about 10 decisions per side-turn, a strong imitation seed from about 17,000 human games, sparse outcomes, tens of dollars of single-GPU rental per experiment, and a prior negative result for Gumbel MCTS with 32 simulations over atomic actions.

---

## Key question 1. Which sample-efficiency tricks reported multipliers, how large, and which apply to a turn-based two-player game with an exact simulator and an imitation seed?

### Takeaway
The only published, per-technique ablation with x-factors is KataGo's (2019): playout cap randomization 1.37x, forced playouts + policy-target pruning 1.25x, global pooling 1.60x, auxiliary opponent-policy target 1.30x, ownership + score targets 1.65x, product about 9.1x, and 50x overall versus ELF OpenGo. Every one of these is defined on a perfect-information two-player game with an exact simulator and transfers structurally to the target; the two largest (value-side auxiliary targets and the network's global-context path) do not depend on search budget at all. Later KataGo methods add 30-90 Elo each (subtree value bias, variance-scaled cPUCT plus uncertainty-weighted playouts, optimistic policy), and MuZero Reanalyse and EfficientZero report order-of-magnitude data reductions, but the latter two are measured on Atari with learned models, not on exact-simulator board games.

### Cited Findings

KataGo (Wu, 2019; arXiv v3 with the ablation table)
- Headline: KataGo's 19-day run on fewer than 30 V100 GPUs (about 1.4 GPU-years) surpassed ELF OpenGo's final model, which used 2,000 V100s for 13-14 days (about 74 GPU-years), a factor of about 50; versus Leela Zero the paper claims at least an order of magnitude. — [Wu 2019, ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1902.10565); [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10565)
- Ablation table (Table 2, verbatim numbers; runs of about 2 days each, identical parameters except the removed component; factor = approximate increase in training time to reach the same Elo): main run 1329 Elo, 1.00x; remove playout cap randomization 1242 Elo, 1.37x; remove forced playouts and policy target pruning 1276 Elo, 1.25x; remove global pooling 1153 Elo, 1.60x; remove auxiliary policy targets 1255 Elo, 1.30x; remove auxiliary ownership and score targets 1139 Elo, 1.65x; remove game-specific features and optimizations 1168 Elo, 1.55x. The paper states the product of the factors is about 9.1x and calls the factors approximate and based on shorter runs. — [Wu 2019, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1902.10565)
- Playout cap randomization: with probability p a move gets a full search of N playouts (and is recorded as a policy-training sample), otherwise a fast search of n playouts (value-training sample only). Main run: p = 0.25, (N, n) = (600, 100) initially, annealed to (1000, 200) after two days. Stated purpose: relieve the tension between policy training (wants many playouts per position) and value training (wants many games). — [Wu 2019, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1902.10565)
- Forced playouts: each root child gets at least n_forced(c) = sqrt(k * P(c) * sum_c' N(c')) playouts with k = 2, so Dirichlet-noised moves are actually explored; policy target pruning then subtracts up to n_forced playouts from children before forming the policy target, so exploration noise does not contaminate the training target. — [Wu 2019, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1902.10565)
- Global pooling: per channel, the mean, the mean scaled by board width, and the max, giving 3c values from c channels, inserted in 2-3 residual blocks and in the policy head. — [Wu 2019, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1902.10565)
- Auxiliary losses and weights: opponent's-next-move policy head with weight w_opp = 0.15; ownership head with weight w_o = 1.5 / b^2 (b = board width); score-belief PDF and CDF losses with weights 0.02 each. — [Wu 2019, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1902.10565)
- Run statistics: 241 million training samples, 4.2 million games, at most 28 V100s; nets grown b6c96 (0.75 days) -> b10c128 (1.75 days) -> b15c192 (7.5 days) -> b20c256 (19 days). — [Wu 2019, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1902.10565)
- Search utility beyond win/loss: u = u_win + c_score * (2/pi) * arctan((x - x0)/b) on the predicted score x, c_score 0.5 then 0.4, x0 re-centred at the root's expected score; motivated as an incentive for cheap, likely score gains without rewarding long-shot large gains. — [Wu 2019, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1902.10565)

KataGo post-paper methods (lightvector, living document, 2020-2024)
- Subtree value bias correction (online-learned per-pattern correction of net values relative to deeper subtree averages): 30 to 60 Elo. — [KataGoMethods.md](https://github.com/lightvector/KataGo/blob/master/docs/KataGoMethods.md)
- Dynamic variance-scaled cPUCT plus uncertainty-weighted MCTS playouts (auxiliary heads predict the value/score error; playouts are down-weighted by predicted uncertainty): about 75 Elo over the preceding release, about 50 Elo if the old cPUCT is optimally tuned. — [KataGoMethods.md](https://github.com/lightvector/KataGo/blob/master/docs/KataGoMethods.md)
- Optimistic policy head (a second policy head trained on the same targets but weighted toward samples where the short-term outcome beat the prediction; used for both sides in search): 40 to 90 Elo depending on other settings. — [KataGoMethods.md](https://github.com/lightvector/KataGo/blob/master/docs/KataGoMethods.md)
- Auxiliary soft policy target (an extra head predicting policy^(1/T) with T about 4, weighted 8x the nominal policy weight): described as greatly speeding policy learning; no number given. — [KataGoMethods.md](https://github.com/lightvector/KataGo/blob/master/docs/KataGoMethods.md)
- Policy surprise weighting (over-sample training rows whose search policy diverged from the prior, 50% uniform / 50% proportional to KL): described as one of the larger improvements between run g170 and earlier runs; no number given. — [KataGoMethods.md](https://github.com/lightvector/KataGo/blob/master/docs/KataGoMethods.md)
- Short-term value and score targets (exponentially averaged future search values at horizons of 6, 16, 50 turns): slightly faster training and slightly better value/score loss; no number. Shaped Dirichlet noise: not validated as a measurable improvement. Root policy softmax temperature 1.25 decaying to 1.1: no number. Fixup initialization: 1.6-1.8x training-speed gain on GPU, later superseded. — [KataGoMethods.md](https://github.com/lightvector/KataGo/blob/master/docs/KataGoMethods.md)

Leela Chess Zero training-side tricks (project write-ups, 2019-2020)
- Self-play uses 800 nodes per move on average; KLD-gain thresholding (introduced in run T50) measures how the root visit distribution changes as search proceeds and stops early on obvious moves while spending more on positions where the policy prediction was poor, keeping the average near 800; no Elo effect is stated in the sources reached. — [lczero.org blog, June 2019](https://lczero.org/blog/2019/06/whats-going-on-with-training/); [lczero.org technical explanation, glossary entry "KLD"](https://lczero.org/dev/wiki/technical-explanation-of-leela-chess-zero/)
- Other run changes listed in the same post: WDL (win/draw/loss) value head instead of a scalar; AlphaZero-style 8x8x73 from-square policy head instead of a flat 1858-move list; training only legal moves; a "Q ratio" mixing search Q with the game result as the value target; only one of the experimental runs (test50) showed a strength gain versus baseline, unquantified. — [lczero.org blog, June 2019](https://lczero.org/blog/2019/06/whats-going-on-with-training/)
- Moves-left head: predicts remaining game length; loss weight 0.1 (with 1.0 for policy and value) did not hurt policy or value; 0.2 hurt policy slightly. — [lczero-training PR #84](https://github.com/LeelaChessZero/lczero-training/pull/84)

MuZero Reanalyse / MuZero Unplugged (Schrittwieser et al., 2021)
- Reanalyse re-runs the (model-based) search on stored trajectories to produce fresh policy and value targets; the reanalyse fraction (share of targets from stored versus fresh data) ranges 0% (online) to 100% (fully offline). Ms. Pac-Man final score improved roughly logarithmically in data as the reanalyse fraction rose; Atari medians as extracted: 1,006.4% at 200M frames, 126.6% at 20M frames; RL Unplugged offline Atari median 265.3% versus CRR 155.6% and BCQ 107.5%. Simulations per move for reanalyse are not stated in the extracted text, and the paper reports nothing on board games. — [Schrittwieser et al. 2021, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2104.06294); [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06294)

EfficientZero (Ye et al., 2021; Atari 100k, learned model)
- Headline: 194.3% mean / 109.0% median human-normalized score at 100k environment steps (about 2 hours of play), versus DQN's 220% mean / 96% median with 500x more data; 50 simulations per move; about 4 GPUs for 7 hours per run. Ablation (median/mean): full 1.090/1.943; without the self-supervised consistency loss 0.340/0.881; without the value-prefix head 0.552/1.482; without model-based off-policy correction 0.836/1.475. — [Ye et al. 2021, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2111.00210)

Sampled MuZero (Hubert et al., 2021)
- Search over K actions sampled from the prior with a beta-hat/beta correction; Go 19x19 with K = 15, 25, 50, 100: K = 50 already closely approaches the all-actions baseline (362 moves), evaluated at 800 simulations; continuous control: 50 simulations per move, K = 20 primary, K as low as 3-5 still learns (Humanoid 21D plateau near K = 10); multi-dimensional actions use a factored policy (one categorical per dimension). — [Hubert et al. 2021, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2104.06303)

Other reported multipliers on exact-simulator board games
- PCZero (Zhao, Tu, Xu, ICML 2022): adding a path-consistency regularizer to AlphaZero raised the win rate against the 2015 Hex Computer Olympiad champion on 13x13 Hex from 84.3% (AlphaZero) to 94.1%, using 900k self-play games; also applied to Othello and Gomoku and in an offline setting. — [PMLR abstract](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v162/zhao22h.html)
- Go-Exploit (Trudeau and Buro, 2023): starting self-play games from an archive of previously visited or searched states instead of the initial position; Connect Four (100 simulations per move) and 9x9 Go (400 per move) on 4 A100s with 700 actors; at equal learning steps the variants beat plain AlphaZero head-to-head 0.593-0.643 (Connect Four, step 600) and 0.641-0.670 (9x9 Go, step 900), and completed about twice the trajectories per learning step (323 vs 147; 147 vs 75). — [Trudeau and Buro 2023, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2302.12359)
- KLENT (2026 preprint): a KL + entropy regularized policy-optimization target; on Animal Shogi, Gardner chess, 9x9 Go, Hex, Othello, Gumbel AlphaZero needed 300M simulator evaluations to reach a 50% average win rate versus 75M for KLENT (4x); with test-time MCTS at 800M evaluations 77.2% versus 53.6%; the gain concentrated on the higher-branching games. Preprint, not yet peer-reviewed. — [arXiv 2602.10894](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.10894)
- Polygames (Cazenave et al., 2020): fully convolutional policy heads (no fully connected layer), global pooling in the value head so a 13x13 Hex model was immediately strong at 19x19, a pool of ten historical models sampled by Elo for opponents, and a rule that a replay-buffer example is seen at most 8 times; no compute figures and no per-technique numbers. — [Cazenave et al. 2020, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2001.09832)
- MiniZero (Wu et al., IEEE ToG 2024): 3-block nets, 2,000 games per iteration, 300 iterations, 60k training steps on 4 GTX 1080Ti; progressive simulation (raise the Gumbel budget from 2 to 200 during training) ends stronger than fixed-budget Gumbel on 9x9 Go and 8x8 Othello but is weaker before 54k training steps. — [Wu et al. 2024, arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.11305v2)

### Inferences
- Structural transfer to the target: playout cap randomization, policy target pruning, global pooling, policy surprise weighting, soft policy targets and the value-side auxiliary heads are all definable on the target's exact simulator and two-player structure. Their KataGo x-factors were measured from scratch in Go; none was measured on an imitation-seeded run, so treat them as upper bounds on the target.
- The two largest KataGo levers (1.65x for ownership/score, 1.60x for global pooling) improve the value function and the network's global context, not the search; they are the levers most likely to matter when search is shallow, because a 1-2 action lookahead is only as good as the leaf values (see question 2).
- Playout cap randomization is the one trick that directly addresses the target's budget: policy targets from 25% of positions at the full budget, value samples from all. Lc0's KLD-gain thresholding is the adaptive version of the same idea.
- Reanalyse and EfficientZero's gains were measured on Atari with learned models; the reanalyse idea (re-search stored positions with the current net to refresh targets) is applicable with an exact simulator and cheap, but no board-game multiplier was found.
- Go-Exploit's start-state archive and PCZero's regularizer are cheap to implement and were measured on exact-simulator games at 100-400 simulations, but neither was tested at the target's 32-simulation regime or from an imitation seed (speculative transfer).

### Gaps
- No paper found that measures these tricks on an imitation-seeded run; all multipliers are from-scratch.
- MuZero Reanalyse's per-move simulation budget and any board-game result: not in the paper.
- MiniZero's "about 200 GTX 1080Ti GPU-hours for Gumbel AlphaZero at 64 simulations" appeared only in a search snippet; it is in neither the README nor docs/Training.md as fetched, so it is unsourced.
- Lc0's KLD-gain threshold values and any Elo/efficiency measurement: the Medium write-up returned HTTP 403; the lczero blog gives the mechanism only.

## Key question 2. With a strong prior and few simulations, when does tree search fail to improve the prior, and what do Gumbel-based methods guarantee? Are there results on search over factored / multi-unit turns rather than atomic actions?

### Takeaway
UCT/AlphaZero visit-count targets are not a policy improvement when simulations are fewer than actions (Danihelka's counterexample), and plain MuZero fails to learn at 16 or fewer simulations in 9x9 Go and at 4 or fewer on Ms. Pac-Man; Gumbel (top-k sampling + Sequential Halving + completed Q-values) guarantees an improvement in expectation only under the premise that the visited actions' Q-values are correct, and learns at 2 simulations. For multi-action turns, every study that compared them (Hero Academy 2016-2018, microRTS 2013, Prismata 2015, Arimaa 2015, Diplomacy 2020-2023) found that tree search over atomic actions collapses as actions-per-turn grow and that searching over whole-turn candidates (sampled, scripted or evolved) is what works; no AlphaZero-style result on whole-turn search in a two-player perfect-information wargame was found.

### Cited Findings

The guarantee and its premise (Danihelka, Guez, Schrittwieser, Silver, ICLR 2022; quotations checked against the first author's 2022 UCL thesis, which reproduces the paper)
- Counterexample: with Q-values (0, 0, 1) and prior (0.5, 0.3, 0.2), picking the best of the top-2 most-probable actions has expected value 0, below the prior's 0.2; the thesis states that when simulations are fewer than actions, UCT and AlphaZero do not ensure a policy improvement. — [Danihelka thesis, UCL 2022, section 5.3.2](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10167022/2/ivo_danihelka_thesis.pdf); [paper on OpenReview](https://openreview.net/forum?id=bERaNdoegnO)
- Algorithm: sample m actions without replacement with the Gumbel-Top-k trick on g + logits; run Sequential Halving over them with n simulations; select argmax of g(a) + logits(a) + sigma(q_hat(a)); the proof shows E[q(A_{n+1})] >= E_{A ~ pi}[q(A)], i.e. improvement in expectation, for any n, under the premise that the visited actions' values are correctly evaluated. The mctx README states the same premise. — [Danihelka thesis, Appendix C.1](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10167022/2/ivo_danihelka_thesis.pdf); [mctx README](https://github.com/google-deepmind/mctx)
- sigma(q_hat) = (c_visit + max_b N(b)) * c_scale * q_hat with c_visit = 50, c_scale = 1.0 for Go and chess (any c_visit >= 50 behaved similarly), c_scale = 0.1 on Atari; the "completed Q-values" give unvisited actions zero advantage so the improved-policy training target softmax(logits + sigma(completed q)) is itself a provable improvement; without completed Q-values the TRPO/MPO variants failed to improve. — [Danihelka thesis, sections 5.4, 5.7.1, C.5](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10167022/2/ivo_danihelka_thesis.pdf)
- Empirical: on 9x9 Go, training with n in {2, 4, 16, 32, 200} (evaluation at 800), MuZero fails to learn from 16 or fewer simulations while Gumbel MuZero learns reliably even with 2; m = min(n, 16) sampled actions in all Go runs, m = 4 learned more slowly; on 19x19 Go and chess at n = 400 Gumbel matches or exceeds MuZero/AlphaZero; on Ms. Pac-Man MuZero fails at 4 or fewer simulations while Gumbel MuZero learns at n = 2, with n <= 18 using no Sequential Halving at all (selection among the n visited actions), which the thesis takes as evidence that the Gumbel action selection, not halving, is the key ingredient. Elo curves are figures only (Figures 5.2-5.5), so exact values could not be transcribed. — [Danihelka thesis, sections 5.7.1-5.7.3](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10167022/2/ivo_danihelka_thesis.pdf)
- Evaluation with 1 simulation (act with the prior's argmax) versus 2-800 for a net trained at n = 2 is shown in Figure C.4; the thesis also notes that fewer simulations substantially cut the self-play TPUs needed (Table C.1), and that 9x9 Go was not data-limited when using three times more TPUs for self-play than for training. — [Danihelka thesis, Appendix C.5](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10167022/2/ivo_danihelka_thesis.pdf)
- Independent replication (MiniZero, 2024): on 9x9 Go, Gumbel AlphaZero with n = 2 is poor early but ends near n = 16; AlphaZero and MuZero at n = 200 outperform the Gumbel runs at n = 2 and 16; on 8x8 Othello n = 2 equals or slightly beats n = 16; the paper states Gumbel Zero matches AlphaZero/MuZero at the same simulation count. — [Wu et al. 2024, arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.11305v2)

Regularized targets at low budgets (Grill et al., ICML 2020)
- The AlphaZero visit distribution approximates the solution of max_y q^T y - lambda_N KL(pi_theta, y) with lambda_N shrinking in N; at low N it fails because new high-value discoveries need many visits to show in counts, the count ratio has low resolution, and the prior only trains on sampled actions. Using the exact regularized solution pi_bar to act, to learn and to search: on Ms. Pac-Man at 5 simulations all three combined substantially beat MuZero in speed and asymptote, converging by about 50; the paper's extracted statement is that MuZero and the regularized variant reach comparable levels only when N_sim >= 24 (|A| <= 18); at N_sim = 5 the "learn" (target) change drives most of the gain, at 50 the "search" change does; on Cheetah Run at 4 simulations the regularized variant beats MuZero significantly. — [Grill et al. 2020, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2007.12509)

Search with amortized value estimates (Hamrick et al., ICLR 2020)
- SAVE seeds MCTS with a learned Q prior and distills the search Q-values back (cross-entropy between softmaxes of Q_MCTS and Q_theta) alongside Q-learning; trained with 10 simulations. Tightrope (sparse, 95% of actions terminal): SAVE solves it reliably at 10 simulations, PUCT with a prior fails frequently, Q-learning without search converges poorly. Construction tasks: Q-learning plus MCTS only at test time gives small gains; SAVE without the amortization loss falls off at test budget 0 and can get worse with larger test budgets because poor Q-values are used for bootstrapping inside the search; UCT with 1,000 simulations does poorly because of the branching factor. — [Hamrick et al. 2020, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1912.02807)

How much test-time search a fixed prior buys
- Board-game scaling study (Jones, 2021; Hex 3x3-9x9, AlphaZero-style, 64 MCTS nodes, about 500 GPU-hours training + 100 evaluation on RTX 2080 Ti): each extra 10x of train compute removes about 15x of test-time compute, down to a floor of a single-node search; an agent with 2x the compute of its opponent wins about two thirds of games. — [Jones 2021, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2104.03113)
- Test-time-only MCTS on a search-free TD agent (Scheiermann and Konen, 2022): Othello agent trained in under 2 CPU-hours could not beat Edax level 2 alone but beat up to level 7 with a 10,000-iteration MCTS wrapper; Connect Four reached over 80% win rate with 50-100 iterations; 2x2x2 Rubik's from 75% to 100% solved; no configuration where a small number of iterations hurt versus none. — [Scheiermann and Konen 2022, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2204.13307)
- AlphaGo (Silver et al., 2016): the RL policy beat the SL policy head-to-head in more than 80% of games, yet the SL policy was the better MCTS prior in AlphaGo, attributed in the paper to humans selecting a diverse beam of promising moves while RL optimizes for the single best move; the value network derived from the RL policy did perform better. — [AlphaGo Nature paper PDF](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/alphago/AlphaGoNaturePaper.pdf)
- Expert Iteration (Anthony, Tian, Barber, 2017; 9x9 Hex): the expert is MCTS with 10,000 iterations per move guided by the apprentice's policy (UCT bonus proportional to the apprentice prior) and value; ExIt learned faster than REINFORCE per network evaluation; value-and-policy ExIt significantly outperformed policy-only ExIt after about 7.4M positions; final 75.3% versus MoHex 1.0 at 10,000 iterations each, 59.3% versus MoHex at 100,000, 55.6% versus 4-second MoHex with solver; no GPU-hours stated. — [Anthony et al. 2017, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1705.08439)

Search over multi-action turns rather than atomic actions
- Hero Academy (Justesen, Mahlmann, Risi, Togelius, IEEE ToG 2018; 5 action points per turn, about 60 legal atomic actions per decision, so about 60^5 = 7.78e8 turn-level branches): with 6 s per turn on one core, vanilla action-level MCTS ran 258,488 iterations per turn yet its leaves averaged 4.86 plies deep (deepest 6.38), i.e. it barely reached the end of its own 5-action turn, and it evaluated only 201 unique end-of-turn outcomes versus 9,344 for Online Evolutionary Planning (OEP, whole-turn genomes) and 579,912 for an exhaustive greedy-turn search; Table II win rates (row versus column): vanilla MCTS 48.5% vs Greedy Action, 0% vs non-exploring MCTS, 4.5% vs BB-MCTS, 2% vs OEP; non-exploring MCTS (deterministic greedy rollouts, exploration off) 98% vs Greedy Action, 100% vs vanilla, 80% vs BB-MCTS, 58% vs OEP; OEP 90% vs Greedy Action, 98% vs vanilla MCTS, 42% vs non-exploring, 52% vs BB-MCTS; varying action points 1-25 (2 s per turn), OEP won 55% or more against every MCTS variant once turns had 10 or more actions, while the MCTS variants were best at few actions per turn; the authors explain the breakdown by the tree becoming too shallow to reach the opponent's reply and note that assuming unit independence (60 instead of 10^6 branches) is wrong in general. Against 85 human games OEP lost 63.5-80% depending on counting, losing all 16-21 games to self-declared experts. — [Justesen et al. 2018, author PDF](https://sebastianrisi.com/wp-content/uploads/justesen_tciaig17.pdf); [IEEE record](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/8007320/)
- Evolutionary MCTS (Baier and Cowling, CIG 2018) and Flexible-Horizon EMCTS (AIIDE 2018): tree nodes are complete turn sequences (genomes), edges are single-action mutations, with a fast greedy AI as the initializer, repairer and opponent model; per-turn branching estimated at 30^5 to 60^5; FH-EMCTS versus vanilla EMCTS at 1 s per turn and 1,000 games: 85% at 2 actions per turn, 69% at 3, 64% at 4, 62% at 5, then 57-62% up to 15; the best search horizon extended beyond the current turn at low action counts but equalled the turn length at the highest counts; the greedy behaviour model was restricted to the top 10 actions by a static ordering for speed; FH-EMCTS beat EMCTS, greedy OEP, BB-MCTS, non-exploring MCTS and Greedy Action at every tested actions-per-turn. — [Baier and Cowling AIIDE 2018 PDF](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/download/13023/12871/16540); [EMCTS CIG 2018 record](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/8490403/)
- microRTS combinatorial bandits (Ontanon, AIIDE 2013): 10 units with 5 actions each already give 5^10 (about 10^7) joint actions; on the FullGame8x8 map (up to 15 units) the average branching factor was 7,922 and the maximum 623,700; UCT's UCB1 sampling was unsuited to such branching and only epsilon-greedy and Naive-Sampling MCTS (a per-unit "local" bandit plus a global bandit over sampled joint actions, assuming additive unit contributions for exploration only) performed well, with NaiveMCTS the best overall; on the small Melee2vs2 map all methods tied. — [Ontanon 2013 PDF](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/12681/12681-52-16198-1-2-20201228.pdf)
- Hearthstone (Swiechowski, Tajmajer, Janusz, CIG 2018): MCTS with atomic actions as tree nodes plus a once-per-turn scripted "board solver" for the attack sub-turn; a 3-layer value network trained on 3.5M positions from strong MCTS-bot games (0.76 accuracy) raised the win rate versus plain MCTS to 82.0% at 0.5 s per move and 76.0% at 1 s. — [Swiechowski et al. 2018, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1808.04794)
- No-press Diplomacy (Anthony et al., NeurIPS 2020; 10^21-10^64 legal joint actions per turn): Sampled Best Response improves the policy one turn at a time by evaluating a small set of candidate joint orders sampled from the current policy against Monte Carlo samples of opponent joint orders; A2C initialized from the imitation policy did not improve (Paquette et al.'s finding, replicated in Appendix E), while all BRPI variants beat the imitation (DipNet SL) and A2C baselines, but the final agents remained exploitable (best exploiters 48% win rate). — [Anthony et al. 2020, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2006.04635)
- DORA (Bakhtin et al., NeurIPS 2021): each turn, sample N_b = 250 joint orders per player from the autoregressive policy, keep the N_c = 50 most likely, run regret matching (1-step lookahead, value network at the leaves, no rollouts during training; depth-2 rollouts at inference) and use the equilibrium policy and value as targets; double-oracle local modifications add up to 1,000 (training) or 10,000 (inference) candidate actions. Ablations in the 2-player France-vs-Austria variant: transformer encoder 1123 +- 13 Elo versus 898 +- 13 for graph convolutions; double oracle 1023 +- 13 versus 927 +- 13 without; from-scratch DORA scored 78% versus top humans over 26 FvA games; the human-initialized RL variant scored 36.3% +- 2.4% in 1-vs-6 against SearchBot (the equal-share baseline is 14.3%). — [Bakhtin et al. 2021, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2110.02924)
- Diplodocus (Bakhtin et al., ICLR 2023): the same loop with DiL-piKL, a 1-ply equilibrium over up to 30 most-likely candidate joint orders per player regularized toward the human-imitation policy with lambda sampled from {1e-4, 1e-3, 1e-2, 1e-1}; ranked first (Diplodocus-High) and third (Diplodocus-Low) in a 50-game tournament with humans, 27 +- 4% and 26 +- 4% score versus the best human's 25 +- 6%; versus an agent population 28-29% against DORA's 13%; no compute stated. — [Bakhtin et al. 2023, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2210.05492)
- Prismata (Churchill and Buro, AIIDE 2015): turns have millions of legal action combinations; Hierarchical Portfolio Search decomposes a turn into 4 phases (defense, abilities, purchases, breach), each with scripted "partial players" (12 in the Master bot), and runs UCT or alpha-beta over the cross product of scripts rather than atomic actions; 3-second UCT reached Tier 6 (top 25% of the ranked ladder) over about 200 secret ladder games, but expert players beat it 100% of the time; alpha-beta at 3 s beat UCT at 3 s 58.9%, and at 100 ms the two were even (52.1/47.9). — [Churchill and Buro 2015 PDF](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/12787/12787-52-16304-1-2-20201228.pdf)

### Inferences
- The target's negative result (Gumbel-32 over atomic actions, 1-2 actions deep, no improvement over the prior) is consistent with the literature on three counts: (i) Gumbel's guarantee is conditional on correct action values, and a 1-2 atomic-action lookahead inside a 10-decision turn evaluates mid-turn positions with a value head trained on end-of-turn or game-level positions, so the premise is weak exactly where it matters; (ii) Hero Academy showed action-level MCTS with 258k iterations barely reaching the end of a 5-action turn, so at 32 simulations a 10-decision turn is out of reach by construction; (iii) SAVE and Grill show that at 5-10 simulations what improves the policy is the quality of the value/Q estimates and the target formulation, not the search itself.
- Every multi-action-turn success reduces the turn to a small candidate set at the turn level and then searches or equilibrates over candidates: sampled joint orders from an autoregressive policy (Diplomacy, 50 of 250 samples), scripted partial moves (Prismata), evolved whole-turn genomes (Hero Academy), or an expert-move ranker keeping the top 1-20% (Arimaa, question 3). The target's autoregressive per-decision policy can produce whole-turn samples the same way; a "Sampled AlphaZero at the turn level" (K sampled turns, evaluated by the value head, Gumbel selection among K) is the direct analogue of DORA/Sampled MuZero, and the +96 Elo of DORA's double-oracle ablation and the failure of imitation-plus-A2C are the closest published evidence. Speculative transfer: no two-player perfect-information wargame has published this.
- The AlphaGo finding that the RL-improved policy was a worse search prior than the SL policy warns that "policy beats seed head-to-head" and "policy is a better prior" are different measurements; the target's pure-match protocol against the reference already measures the former.
- Jones's 10x-train-for-15x-test rule says a strong prior can be substituted for search, not that search adds nothing; the relevant question for the target is at what turn-level candidate count the value head's ranking beats the prior's argmax, which none of these papers measured for a wargame.

### Gaps
- The Gumbel paper's Elo-versus-simulation numbers exist only as plotted curves (thesis Figures 5.2-5.5, C.4); no table transcribes them.
- No published AlphaZero/Gumbel result was found on search over whole multi-unit turns in a two-player perfect-information wargame; the Diplomacy line is simultaneous-move and multiplayer, Hero Academy and Prismata use no learned policy prior.
- A DTIC report on MCTS for multi-action wargames (AD1134590) returned HTTP 403 and could not be read.
- No source quantifies how much stronger than the prior a 1-ply, K-candidate turn-level search is as a function of K in any game.

## Key question 3. How did games with enormous branching factors (Stratego, Arimaa, multi-unit wargames) structure their action space or search so learning was possible?

### Takeaway
Three patterns recur: (a) factor the turn into sequential sub-decisions with small heads (DeepNash: piece-select then destination, both 100-way; AlphaStar: autoregressive action type, units, target; Diplomacy: per-unit orders decoded sequentially; Generals.io: one cell-move per step over an HxWx9 map); (b) replace enumeration by sampling from the learned policy (Sampled MuZero K = 50 of 362 in Go; Diplomacy 50 of 250 samples; Arimaa's ranker keeping the top 1% which holds 81% of expert moves); (c) drop deep search entirely and rely on a regularized learning dynamic (DeepNash's R-NaD, AlphaStar's league with KL-to-human regularization).

### Cited Findings
- Stratego / DeepNash (Perolat et al., Science 2022): game tree of about 10^535 nodes, 10^66 possible deployments per player; the agent's action space is a discrete 100-way choice (a board square) throughout; deployment is 40 sequential single-piece actions in a fixed piece order; each move is two stages, a piece-selection head then a piece-displacement head, on a U-Net torso with a value head; games are drawn after 2,000 total moves; trained with R-NaD (reward transformation toward a regularization policy, replicator dynamics to a fixed point, then update the regularizer) with no search and no human data; 768 learner and 256 actor TPU nodes, 7.21M steps, batch 768 trajectories; results: 84% win rate over 50 ranked games against human experts on Gravon (3rd all-time and 3rd in the 2022 ranking), at least 97% against eight prior bots; the paper states that with 10^66 deployments belief-tracking search techniques are intractable. — [Perolat et al. 2022, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2206.15378)
- Arimaa / SHARP (Wu, ICGA Journal 2015): about 17,000 legal moves per turn (four steps per turn; 16,567 average in the 2015 test set); root move ordering by a Bradley-Terry model over several thousand binary move features, trained on several thousand games by players rated 2100+; out of sample the expert move is in the top 1% of the ordering 81.0% of the time, top 5% 94.4%, top 10% 97.5%, top 20% 99.2% (top-1 15.0%, top-10 44.4%, top-100 78.3%); moves past the first 10% are searched one step shallower and past 20% two steps shallower; the ranker costs about 0.1 s per position so it is used only at the root; within-tree "tactical move generators" produce about 3% of legal moves yet capture about 97% of the evaluation gain of expert moves over passing, and adding them gave 80 Elo immediately, the largest single search gain in years; a hybrid step/move formulation (move lists mixing 1-4 step moves; movewise branching about 10,000 versus stepwise about 30) gave about 20% speedup at 9-step depth and more when deeper; SHARP gained over 600 Elo in two years and won the 2015 Arimaa Challenge. — [Wu 2015 PDF](https://icosahedral.net/downloads/djwu2015arimaa_color.pdf); [Wu 2011 thesis](http://arimaa.com/arimaa/papers/DavidWu/djwuthesis.pdf)
- StarCraft II / AlphaStar (Vinyals et al., Nature 2019): about 10^26 possible choices per step handled by an autoregressive policy with a pointer network over selected units; supervised seed from 971,000 replays by players above 3,500 MMR (top 22%), then fine-tuned on 16,000 winning replays above 6,200 MMR, raising the win rate versus the built-in Elite bot from 87% to 96% (Protoss vs Protoss); the supervised agent rated 3,699 MMR (top 16% of humans); RL kept a KL penalty toward the supervised policy plus pseudo-rewards for following human build-order statistics, which the paper calls critical (Fig. 3E); each agent trained on 32 TPUv3 for 44 days; final agent above 99.8% of ranked players. — [AlphaStar Nature paper PDF](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- Diplomacy: orders for all of a player's units are decoded sequentially by a relational/LSTM decoder (BRPI 2020; DORA 2021); planning samples whole joint orders from that decoder (N_b = 250, keep N_c = 50 per player) and runs a 1-step-lookahead regret-matching equilibrium over the candidates; see question 2 for numbers. — [Anthony et al. 2020, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2006.04635); [Bakhtin et al. 2021, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2110.02924)
- Generals.io (2025 preprint): actions are (pass, i, j, direction, split) emitted as an HxWx9 map tensor, one unit-move per step; behaviour cloning from 16,320 replays filtered from 347,000 (at least one 70-star player), then PPO self-play with an opponent pool; no search. — [arXiv 2507.06825](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.06825v1)
- Sampled MuZero: for factored/multi-dimensional actions, one categorical per dimension, then K joint samples from the product policy; K = 3-5 suffices on 21-56D control, K = 50 near-full on 362-action Go. — [Hubert et al. 2021, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2104.06303)
- Polygames: policy as a per-location map (fully convolutional), which scales to large boards and transfers across sizes (13x13 to 19x19 Hex). — [Cazenave et al. 2020, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2001.09832)
- Prismata: scripted partial players per phase, search over the cross product (see question 2). — [Churchill and Buro 2015 PDF](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/12787/12787-52-16304-1-2-20201228.pdf)
- microRTS: per-unit local bandits plus a global bandit over sampled joint actions (Naive Sampling). — [Ontanon 2013 PDF](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/12681/12681-52-16198-1-2-20201228.pdf)
- Battle for Wesnoth, prior AI work found: a 2012 machine-learning recruiter (neural nets over faction, gold, terrain shares, village counts, time of day, leader HP, distances) won 66.6% of 2,363 and 69.3% of 1,179 games against the RCA recruiter; a 2013 CIG paper (Wiens, Denzinger, Paskaradevan) learns cooperative unit behaviour with RL over rule weights and evolutionary rule sets (abstract not retrievable; description from the IEEE listing). No search-based or self-play learning paper on Wesnoth was found. — [Wesnoth wiki, Machine Learning Recruiter](https://wiki.wesnoth.org/Machine_Learning_Recruiter); [IEEE Xplore 6633608](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/6633608/)
- Tafl: TaflZero, an AlphaZero-style 11x11 Hnefatafl engine (GitHub project, no paper), reports that a large replay window of millions of positions was the single biggest factor in training quality; informal source. — [TaflZero GitHub](https://github.com/sovelin/taflzero)

### Inferences
- The target already has the DeepNash/AlphaStar-style factorization (actor, type, target, weapon heads); the missing piece relative to Diplomacy/DORA is using that policy as a whole-turn proposal distribution for a turn-level search rather than as an atomic-action prior for a within-turn tree.
- Arimaa gives the quantitative shape of what an expert-move ranker can do to a 17,000-move turn: keep 1% and lose 19% of expert moves, keep 20% and lose under 1%. The target's imitation policy over about 350 atomic actions plays the same role per decision; sampling whole turns from it is the multi-decision analogue, and the coverage number to measure is how often the human turn (or a turn the value head prefers) is among K samples.
- DeepNash and AlphaStar both show that, when search is infeasible, keeping the learning dynamic near a reference policy (R-NaD's regularization policy; AlphaStar's KL to the supervised policy, which AlphaStar's authors call critical) is what made model-free improvement stable; Diplodocus's piKL is the same idea inside a search. For an imitation-seeded target this argues for an explicit KL-to-seed term in any self-play objective (transfer plausible but not measured in a wargame).

### Gaps
- No paper found on Advance Wars, Amazons-with-AlphaZero, or a Wesnoth-like hex wargame with learned search; the miniXCOM MCTS-TD paper (arXiv 2210.05014) surfaced in search but was not read.
- DeepNash's per-turn legal-move count is not stated as a single number in the text (the action head is 100-way per stage).

## Key question 4. What auxiliary targets are exact functions of the game state or trajectory (ownership, score, opponent's next move, units alive) and what did they measurably buy?

### Takeaway
KataGo's ablation is the only per-target measurement on an exact-simulator game: final ownership plus final score (exact functions of the finished game) bought 1.65x in training time, and predicting the opponent's next move (an exact function of the trajectory) bought 1.30x; Lc0's moves-left head (exact remaining game length) and WDL head were adopted with no published Elo, and KataGo's short-term value targets gave only slight gains. Nothing was found measuring a "units alive" or material-trajectory target.

### Cited Findings
- KataGo ownership and score heads: final per-point ownership (weight 1.5/b^2) and a distribution over the final score difference (PDF and CDF losses, weight 0.02 each); removing both cost 1329 -> 1139 Elo in the 2-day ablation, an estimated 1.65x in training time; the score head is also used inside search as a bounded arctan score utility. — [Wu 2019, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1902.10565)
- KataGo opponent-next-move head (weight 0.15): removing it cost 1329 -> 1255 Elo, an estimated 1.30x. — [Wu 2019, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1902.10565)
- KataGo short-term value/score targets (exponentially averaged future search values at 6/16/50 turns): slightly faster training, slightly better value and score loss; auxiliary soft policy target (policy^(1/4), 8x weight): described as a large speedup of policy learning, unquantified; uncertainty heads (predicted value/score error) feed uncertainty-weighted playouts, about 75 Elo together with variance-scaled cPUCT. — [KataGoMethods.md](https://github.com/lightvector/KataGo/blob/master/docs/KataGoMethods.md)
- Lc0 moves-left head: exact remaining plies; loss weight 0.1 alongside 1.0/1.0 did not hurt policy or value, 0.2 hurt policy slightly; WDL head replaces the scalar value; no Elo published in the sources reached. — [lczero-training PR #84](https://github.com/LeelaChessZero/lczero-training/pull/84); [lczero.org blog, June 2019](https://lczero.org/blog/2019/06/whats-going-on-with-training/)
- EfficientZero's largest component is a self-supervised next-state consistency loss (not an exact game-state target) worth 0.340 -> 1.090 median on Atari 100k; its value-prefix head (predict the sum of rewards over the unrolled steps, an exact function of the trajectory) is worth 0.552 -> 1.090. — [Ye et al. 2021, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2111.00210)
- Hearthstone: a winner-prediction value head trained on 3.5M search-bot positions (0.76 accuracy) as the sole learned component raised MCTS win rate to 82% versus plain MCTS. — [Swiechowski et al. 2018, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1808.04794)
- AlphaGo: value-network targets from complete games overfit (successive positions are near-duplicates); the fix was one sampled position per distinct self-play game, 30M positions, giving train/test MSE 0.226/0.234. — [AlphaGo Nature paper PDF](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/alphago/AlphaGoNaturePaper.pdf)
- Diplomacy DORA: value targets are the 1-step equilibrium value rather than the game outcome (a planning-derived, not exact, target). — [Bakhtin et al. 2021, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2110.02924)

### Inferences
- Exact-function analogues available in the target from the simulator: final village ownership per hex (ownership), final gold/unit-value difference or the end-of-game side score (score), the human's or opponent's next decision (opponent policy), remaining turns (moves-left), and per-unit survival at game end (a per-token ownership-like target). Only the first three have published multipliers, all from Go; the survival target is untested (speculative).
- KataGo's value-side heads are dense per-hex signals from sparse outcomes, which is the stated reason they accelerate value learning; the target's sparse-outcome, 17k-game seed regime is where such dense exact targets should matter most, but this is inference, not a measured result.
- The one-position-per-game rule from AlphaGo and the moves-left weight ceiling from Lc0 are the two documented failure modes to respect when adding exact targets: correlated positions and over-weighted auxiliary losses.

### Gaps
- No published measurement of a "units alive" / per-unit survival auxiliary target in any game.
- No Elo figure for Lc0's moves-left or WDL heads was found in primary sources.
- All auxiliary-target multipliers come from from-scratch Go runs; none from imitation-seeded training.

## Key question 5. What is the cheapest published pipeline (compute in GPU-hours) that produced a self-play improvement over a strong imitation prior in a complex game?

### Takeaway
The cheapest documented case is Generals.io (2025 preprint): 3 hours of behaviour cloning plus 36 hours of PPO self-play on one H100 lifted the imitation agent from 1,874 to about 2,052 Elo (roughly +100 from self-play, the rest from reward shaping and population play) and to the top 25 of the human 1v1 ladder, with no search. The canonical case is AlphaGo (2016): 50 GPUs for one day of REINFORCE self-play took the SL policy to an 80% head-to-head win rate over its seed and 85% against Pachi without search. Search-based improvements over an imitation seed (Diplomacy DORA/Diplodocus, AlphaStar) used hundreds of accelerators for weeks, and the only cheap from-scratch AlphaZero-style figures are on small boards (about 500 GPU-hours for the whole Hex scaling study; 4 GTX 1080Ti for MiniZero's 9x9 Go).

### Cited Findings
- Generals.io (2025): behaviour cloning on 16,320 curated replays for 3 hours on one H100, then 36 hours of PPO (GAE lambda 0.95, opponent pool of 3) on the same GPU; BC alone 1,874 Elo, final 2,052 Elo (Human.exe 2,018, Flobot 1,500); 54.82% over 529 games against Human.exe; top 0.003% of the 1v1 leaderboard; self-play contributed about 100 Elo, reward shaping and population training added robustness (78.4 +- 1.8% versus pure self-play); no search. Preprint, single game, action per step is one unit move. — [arXiv 2507.06825](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.06825v1)
- AlphaGo (2016): SL policy from 30M positions / 160k games, 57.0% move prediction (trained about 3 weeks on 50 GPUs); RL policy initialized from it and trained by REINFORCE with a baseline against a pool of past checkpoints for 10,000 minibatches of 128 games on 50 GPUs for one day; result: more than 80% wins versus the SL policy and 85% versus Pachi (100k simulations) with no search; value network then trained for one week on 50 GPUs. — [AlphaGo Nature paper PDF](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/alphago/AlphaGoNaturePaper.pdf)
- Diplomacy BRPI (2020): imitation seed from about 150,000 human games (4-5 points better prediction accuracy than DipNet); A2C from the seed did not improve; BRPI did; compute not stated. — [Anthony et al. 2020, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2006.04635)
- DORA (2021): 192 V100s (4 for training, the rest generating data) for about a week per stage, two stages, for the 2-player variant; the human-initialized 7-player RL variant reached 36.3% in 1-vs-6 against SearchBot. — [Bakhtin et al. 2021, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2110.02924)
- AlphaStar (2019): 32 TPUv3 per agent for 44 days from a supervised seed rated top 16% of humans. — [AlphaStar Nature paper PDF](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)
- Cheap from-scratch AlphaZero-style references (no imitation seed): Hex 3x3-9x9 scaling study, about 500 GPU-hours training on RTX 2080 Ti; MiniZero 9x9 Go / 8x8 Othello with 3-block nets on 4 GTX 1080Ti, 2,000 games x 300 iterations; Go-Exploit on 4 A100s for 600-900 learning steps; Othello TD n-tuple agent under 2 CPU-hours then test-time MCTS; KataGo 1.4 V100-years for superhuman 19x19 Go. — [Jones 2021, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2104.03113); [Wu et al. 2024, arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.11305v2); [Trudeau and Buro 2023, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2302.12359); [Scheiermann and Konen 2022, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2204.13307); [Wu 2019, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1902.10565)
- Student of Games (2023): trained with TPU resources similar to AlphaZero's 3,500 concurrent TPUv4 actors, and still substantially weaker than AlphaZero in Go (0.5% wins) and chess, which the authors attribute to MCTS being more efficient than CFR-style search in perfect-information games. — [Schmid et al., ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2112.03178)
- Hearthstone (2018): a value head from 3.5M search-bot positions, no self-play RL, gave 76-82% over plain MCTS; compute not stated. — [Swiechowski et al. 2018, ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1808.04794)

### Inferences
- Both cheap imitation-seed improvements (AlphaGo's RL policy, Generals.io) are search-free policy-gradient self-play against a pool of past checkpoints, measured by head-to-head win rate, at 40-1,200 GPU-hours of 2016-2025 hardware. Neither is a two-player turn-based wargame and Generals.io is a preprint; the Diplomacy line shows the same recipe (imitation + A2C) failing in a multi-unit simultaneous-move game, so the transfer is uncertain in both directions.
- AlphaGo's caveat (the RL policy was a worse MCTS prior than the SL policy) means a cheap policy-gradient improvement can be real at the raw-policy level and still not help a later search stage.
- For the target's budget (tens of dollars, one GPU), the published evidence supports: (i) exact auxiliary targets on the imitation trainer (question 4), which cost no self-play; (ii) a search-free self-play leg with a KL-to-seed term and a checkpoint pool, sized like AlphaGo's day (about 1.3M games there; the target's game cost is the binding number); (iii) turn-level sampled search only if the value head is first shown to rank sampled turns better than the prior's argmax (question 2). Items (ii) and (iii) are inferences; no paper ran them on a hex wargame.

### Gaps
- No published GPU-hour figure for a search-based self-play improvement over an imitation seed in any complex game under about 1,000 GPU-hours.
- ExIt, BRPI and Diplodocus do not state compute; Sampled MuZero and Gumbel state TPU counts only qualitatively.
- Generals.io's numbers are from a 2025 arXiv preprint and were not independently confirmed.
- Stochastic MuZero's numeric results (2048 score, backgammon ply-level comparison, simulation counts) could not be retrieved: OpenReview is CAPTCHA-gated, the ICLR page and a secondary review give only qualitative claims (matches or exceeds prior methods on 2048 and backgammon, parity with MuZero on Go). Its relevance to the target is limited in any case, since the target's simulator is exact and combat randomness can be sampled directly.
