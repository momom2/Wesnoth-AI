# Human data densifies the win-loss signal

No strategy-game agent that reached strong play replaced win/loss as its outcome reward; every one made the *signal* dense with information that does not come from the outcome bit. AlphaStar's own ablation is the cleanest ladder: **149 test Elo from scratch, 936 supervised only, 1020 with supervised initialisation, 1400 adding a KL penalty toward the supervised policy, 1540 adding human-derived strategy statistics**, and its critic that sees the opponent's state during training moved best-response win rate from **22% to 82%** ([AlphaStar Nature](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)). The same corpus-derived levers recur in every cheaper reproduction (TStarBot-X, SCC, DI-star, the Lux and microRTS winners), and the value-side levers that need no search at all carry the largest published multipliers: KataGo's exact ownership-and-score heads were worth **1.65x in training time**, its six tricks **9.1x in product** ([KataGo](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1902.10565)). Where search helped on multi-action turns, it was search over a *small set of sampled whole turns* anchored to an imitation policy, not over atomic actions: piKL took a Diplomacy imitation policy from **7.9% to 32.9% population score at inference with no training** ([piKL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.07544)), while every study of atomic-action MCTS on multi-action turns found it collapsing (Hero Academy: 258,488 iterations per turn reaching 4.86 plies, 0-4.5% win rates). The dedicated credit-assignment literature (RUDDER, HCA, CCA, TVT, Synthetic Returns) has no two-player result at all, and group baselines (RLOO, GRPO) were never run on binary outcomes near p = 0.5, where the arithmetic demands about 100 paired samples per state to detect a 0.05 effect. For this project the options with measured effects that touch only the loss, the label or the decode, and are therefore verifiable by one 800-game match, are: the KL-to-seed anchor, a god-view training-time critic, a winner/high-rating fine-tune, an offline actor-critic pass on the 17,000 games (AlphaStar Unplugged: behaviour cloning 84% to 93-98%), KataGo-style exact auxiliary heads, terminal relabelling of capped games (Lux: 50% to 85% from a ±√margin stage), and KL-regularised turn-level candidate search. The cheapest self-play that beat an imitation seed anywhere was search-free policy gradient with a checkpoint pool and an anchor (AlphaGo: one day on 50 GPUs, 80% over its seed; Generals.io: 36 H100-hours, about +100 Elo), and its documented failure, A2C from an imitation seed in Diplomacy, has the shape of this project's own legs. Every transfer below is speculative on effect size; the ledger at the end separates what was measured, and where, from what is inferred.

## Every top agent kept win/loss and bought density from the human corpus

AlphaStar's reward is the undiscounted match outcome, -1/0/+1 at the end, and the paper names reward sparsity, the need for diverse strategies and long-horizon off-policy learning as the three obstacles. Its answer was to make human data the exploration device: initialise from the supervised policy, keep minimising the KL divergence to it throughout RL, and sample a strategy statistic z (the first 20 built units and buildings plus a presence vector over units, buildings, effects and upgrades) from human games, paying pseudo-rewards for the edit distance between sampled and executed build order and the Hamming distance between cumulative statistics, each active with probability 25% and each with its own value function and loss. The ablation puts numbers on each piece: **KL anchoring is worth +380 Elo over bare initialisation, the z statistics +140 more**, main exploiters +153 and league exploiters +131 on top, and the off-policy stack (V-trace to +TD(λ) to +UPGO) moves best-response win rate **49% to 73% to 82%** ([AlphaStar Nature](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)). The league bookkeeping is domain-independent and also measured: prioritised fictitious self-play plus self-play keeps a **71% minimum win rate against all past versions where plain self-play holds 46%**, the caption reading "Naive self-play has high Elo, but is more forgetful" ([AlphaStar Nature](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)). The authors' generality note is the caveat that matters here: the latent z is general, but "the particular choice of statistics required domain knowledge".

The two small-compute reproductions tell the same story from the other side. TStarBot-X ran a faithful AlphaStar league at 1/30 the consuming speed and 1/73 the generating speed and watched it collapse: the main agent converged to Zergling Rush or Roach Push within days in several repetitions, which the authors attribute to insufficient exploration ([TStarBot-X](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.13729)). Their fixes are all targeted signal rather than global shaping: rule-guided policy search distils six if-else rules (morph Lair after the opponent does, build a Lurker den after seeing a Lurker) as one-hot targets at critical states, motivated by the observation that such a decision "would probably only be performed once among thousands of steps"; and DAPO replaces the fixed KL-to-supervised anchor with a KL to the *previous* checkpoint, active only in the first four minutes of a game, because their supervised agent was Platinum-level and "consistently restricting MA around the supervised agent prevents MA from improving efficiently". Switching DAPO on for the last 15 of 57 days "significantly improved" every agent ([TStarBot-X](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.13729)). SCC hit the complementary wall: exploiters trained from the supervised agent on sparse win/loss could not discover a reaper rush, so it initialised exploiters from the *current main agent* and added a dense reward for following a chosen z, exploiting the fact that different z gave the conditioned main agent 35% to 65% win rates against its unconditioned self ([SCC](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.13169)). SCC also froze the policy and trained only the value network for the first 50 steps of RL, a warm-up the paper reports without an ablation.

What this fixes for Wesnoth is the ordering of levers. The anchor is the load-bearing item and it is a loss term, so it costs one leg and one match; its one documented failure mode is becoming a ceiling once the policy passes the seed, and the documented remedy (anchor to the previous checkpoint, early game only) is a config change. The z analogue is the first N recruits and the set of unit types recruited, with edit and Hamming distances as pseudo-rewards; a config-selected z is a forced opener, which is one of the project's stated customisation goals, and SCC's branching is the affordable way to manufacture an exploiter without waiting for sparse outcomes to find a strategy. Both are measured in SC2 only, and no paper reports a KL-coefficient sweep or the point at which the anchor binds.

## Shaped rewards helped against weak opponents and were dropped at the top

The record on hand-shaped rewards is consistent across eight systems, and it is the record the project's eval contract already encodes. SC2LE's Blizzard-score agents "converged to simply preserving the initial mining process without building further units or structures", and no baseline won a single full game on win/loss ([SC2LE](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1708.04782)). Pang and Liu found win/loss "relatively poor" on their two-layer hierarchy at difficulty 7 against a designed reward, then at the cheating levels found the designed reward slower at first but better, and their final three-layer agent uses the outcome reward alone ([Liu 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.11553)). Lee's modular agent used the change in supply difference because win/loss "provides too sparse training signals" and capped at **57-60% against Very Hard** ([Lee 2018](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1811.03555)); LastOrder in Brood War reports **0% against Ximp without reward shaping** ([LastOrder](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1812.00336)). OpenAI Five's shaped reward was "modeled loosely after potential-based shaping functions, though the guarantees therein do not apply", and the authors note the agent's style "is likely influenced by our shaped reward function" ([OpenAI Five](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.06680)).

The affordable platforms make the same choice explicit as a schedule. Gym-microRTS reached state of the art in about 60 GPU-hours with a hand-weighted reward (win +10, harvest +1, worker +1, combat unit +4) and shows the shaped return rising smoothly while the sparse win/loss return stays high-variance, flagging that shaped rewards can raise shaped return without winning ([Gym-microRTS](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2105.13807)). RAISocketAI, the first deep-RL winner of the microRTS competition, keeps three value heads (shaped, terminal win/loss, cost-weighted unit difference) and schedules their weights from [0.8, 0.01, 0.19] to [0, 0.99, 0.01] across 300M steps, ending "only [on] win-loss sparse rewards" ([RAISocketAI](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.08112v1)); a 2026 successor thesis contains "a formal analysis of a discount-induced reward collapse under shaped-to-sparse annealing" ([UECD](https://github.com/mathisdelsart/microrts-drl-uecd)). The Lux AI curriculum is the one multi-unit turn-based result with a per-stage number: **pure win/loss from scratch 0%, dense shaping 50%, adding an end-of-game ±√|city margin| reward 85%, finishing on ±1 win/loss above 90%**, at 600 CPU cores plus one V100 and about 5M episodes for the first phase ([Lux, Chen 2023](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2301.01609)). The cost side is quantified once: NRL's potential-based shaping in a capture-the-flag game cut convergence time by 50% to 87.5% but lowered final score against the hard attacker by about 20% until the shaping gradients were doubled ([NRL CTF](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.16339)). CivRealm's full-game PPO on the civilisation score shows the other failure: score rising while the strategy stays myopic after 2M steps ([CivRealm](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.10568)). Atlatl, the NPS hex wargame, engineers the sparse problem away with a per-step score difference, and still every single RL model at 10M steps scored below every scripted model (**best RL 21.8 against the worst script's 43.0 over 100,000 games**) ([Atlatl dissertation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.13333)).

The transferable pattern is "shape early, anneal to outcome, keep an outcome head throughout", and the one piece of it that costs no new games is the label. Lux's ±√margin stage is a terminal relabel, and the project's capped games (where ±1 is undefined) are exactly where a bounded signed function of final material or village margin would land; stored records can be relabelled without generation. Its evaluation opponent is unnamed in the summary and no source reports the effect of relabelling capped games, so the transfer is speculative and the match is the verdict. A second corroboration is behavioural: a Fire Emblem-like tactics paper paid +0.3 per valid action as a crude "act more" bonus ([Mirror Mode](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.11902)), TStarBot-X downsampled the do-nothing class in imitation, and the project's own end_turn logit offset produced about +229 Elo at decode time; these agree that acting more is a known lever, not that the reward form matters.

## The corpus holds about 17,000 outcome bits and ten million demonstrated decisions

A binary outcome carries at most one bit per game (exactly one at p = 0.5), and Arumugam, Henderson and Bacon argue that "it is not the sparsity of the reward itself that causes difficulty in credit assignment, but rather the information sparsity", defined as the mutual information between the action at a state and the return ([Arumugam 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.06224)). AlphaGo's value network, trained on complete KGS games, "memorized the game outcomes rather than generalizing to new positions", test MSE 0.37 against 0.19 on training, because successive positions share one label; the fix was **30 million positions, one per distinct self-play game**, giving 0.226/0.234 ([AlphaGo](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/alphago/AlphaGoNaturePaper.pdf)). KataGo states the same limit as "only one noisy binary result per entire game" ([KataGo](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1902.10565)). By that arithmetic 17,019 games hold at most about 17,000 outcome bits for a value head, while the same games hold roughly 10^7 demonstrated decisions each worth up to log2(350) ≈ 8.5 bits; this is arithmetic on the project's numbers, not a published result, but it is why every offline method that ignored the actions lost to the one that kept them.

AlphaStar Unplugged is the direct measurement of what the outcome bit in a human corpus is worth. On 2.8M StarCraft II games with outcomes and MMR, behaviour cloning wins **84% against the very_hard bot; every Q-function method is "very unstable" at 0%; CRR, BCQ, CWP and R-BVE sit at 83-84%, no gain; return-conditioned BC "seemed to ignore the return conditioning"**; the methods that worked "first train a model to estimate the behavior policy and behavior value function. Then, use the behavior value function to improve the policy", keeping the target policy close to the behaviour policy ([Unplugged](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.03526)). Table 5's reference agents read BC 88%, fine-tuned BC 95%, MuZero-supervised 92% (95% with MCTS at inference), offline actor-critic 98%, with robustness 25% to 36% to 43% and Elo 1380 to 1485 to 1548 ([Unplugged](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.03526)). The paper's warning is the operative risk: "the winner of a game between two low-skilled players would consistently lose to the loser of a game between two professional players", so outcome-as-label needs the rating; their mitigation is to feed MMR as an input and set it to the maximum at inference.

The filtering result is the most-replicated corpus-side gain in the survey. Unplugged: full corpus above MMR 3500 gives 84%; restricting the *whole* training set to MMR above 6000 gives **65%**, to wins only **51%**; full corpus followed by fine-tuning on 21,836 winning episodes above 6,200 MMR gives **89%**, and 10% of the episodes gets close to the best ([Unplugged](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.03526)). AlphaStar's fine-tune on 16,000 winning replays above 6,200 MMR took Elite-bot win rate from **87% to 96%** ([AlphaStar Nature](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)); SCC's MMR-5300 subset peaked about 10 points above its MMR-4300 set and a final fine-tune on 3,458 winning replays above 5,500 reached 97% ([SCC](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.13169)); DI-star's 7,000-replay fine-tune moved **87% to 97%** ([DI-star guide](https://github.com/opendilab/DI-star/blob/main/docs/guidance_to_small_scale_training.md)). Four groups, one recipe: train on everything, then a low-learning-rate pass on winners at the top of the rating distribution. The imitation-stage lever with the largest single reported effect is TStarBot-X's importance sampling: downsample NOOP by 0.2 and the generic Smart click by 0.25, upsample abilities that appear less than once per replay (capped at 10x), reweight the loss to stay unbiased, and Elite-bot win rate goes **68% to 85%** ([TStarBot-X](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.13729)).

For Wesnoth each of these is one imitation-recipe pass plus one 800-game match, with three cautions from the project's own record. The 2026-09-08 contamination review found the seed's value head trained on outcomes of holdout games, so any offline-RL or fine-tune pass must split by the corpus manifest. Holdout CE is not a strength proxy here (half an epoch more of the seed's own recipe cost 111 ± 13 Elo at an equal holdout CE), so the match is the only verdict. And all four fine-tune replications are SC2; no paper measures the recipe on a 17,000-game corpus or a turn-based game, so the size is speculative even though the direction is the best-replicated in the survey.

## Exact auxiliary targets and privileged critics are the value-side levers with numbers

KataGo's ablation is the only per-technique table on an exact-simulator two-player game, and it is the one place a from-scratch AlphaZero run was made cheap: 19 days on fewer than 30 V100s (about 1.4 GPU-years) surpassed ELF OpenGo's 74 GPU-years, a factor of about 50. Removing each component from the 1329-Elo two-day baseline: **playout cap randomization 1242 (1.37x), forced playouts and policy-target pruning 1276 (1.25x), global pooling 1153 (1.60x), the auxiliary opponent-next-move head 1255 (1.30x), the ownership and score heads 1139 (1.65x)**, product about 9.1x ([KataGo](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1902.10565)). The two largest levers touch the value function and the network's global context, not the search: the ownership head predicts final per-point ownership (weight 1.5/b²), the score head a distribution over the final score difference (PDF and CDF losses, 0.02 each), and the paper's stated reason they help "even up through the ends of those runs" is that the binary outcome "is a direct function of finer variables" that are dense per-point signals. Later KataGo methods add 30 to 60 Elo (subtree value bias), about 75 (variance-scaled cPUCT with uncertainty-weighted playouts from heads that predict the value error) and 40 to 90 (an optimistic policy head) ([KataGoMethods](https://github.com/lightvector/KataGo/blob/master/docs/KataGoMethods.md)). An earlier Go result points the same way: a value network trained on many komi settings plus board ownership won **67.6%** head-to-head against the plain value network ([multi-labelled value nets](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.10701)). The two documented failure modes are over-weighting and correlation: Lc0's moves-left head at weight 0.1 did not hurt policy or value while 0.2 hurt policy slightly ([Lc0 PR 84](https://github.com/LeelaChessZero/lczero-training/pull/84)), and AlphaGo's one-position-per-game rule above.

The privileged-critic result is the largest single-mechanism effect in the whole survey. AlphaStar feeds the opponent's observations to the value functions "to decrease variance of the value estimates", training-time only since "value functions are unnecessary during evaluation", and the ablation reads **22% without opponent information, 82% with** ([AlphaStar Nature](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)). Counterfactual Credit Assignment gives the condition under which such conditioning is unbiased and no higher in variance: the baseline's extra input must be independent of the agent's action given the state, and the paper names the valid inputs explicitly, "actions of other agents at the same time step ... the full state (at time t) of the simulator ... or the opponent's private observations (at time t) in Starcraft" ([CCA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.09464)). The failure it guards against is learned helplessness: a hindsight baseline that is not action-independent makes "the agent ... assume the outcome is entirely controlled by chance". COMA is the per-unit version, a centralised critic marginalising one unit's action while holding the rest of the joint action fixed, worth 4 to 10 win-rate points over the same critic with a plain V baseline on 3-5-unit StarCraft micro (87 vs 83, 81 vs 71, 82 vs 76, 47 vs 39) ([COMA](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08926)), though a 2025 benchmark reports COMA at 2-7% on SMACv2 maps where MAPPO reaches 42-57% ([MACA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.06836)), so the critic must be accurate enough to rank alternatives. Common random numbers are the one variance lever the sources prove and an exact simulator makes free: TRPO's vine estimator resets to a rollout state, samples K actions and evaluates each under "the same random number sequence for the noise", which "greatly reduce[s] the variance of the Q-value differences" ([TRPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.05477)), and a 2026 paper gives a provable variance reduction for relative utility when paired simulations share a rollout policy beyond some depth ([CRN rollouts](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.04732)).

The Wesnoth analogues are exact functions of the simulator's records: final village ownership per hex, final gold or unit-value margin, the human's or opponent's next decision, turns remaining, and per-unit survival at game end. The project's own principle forbids god view only in the policy's legality mask, so a critic that sees both sides with fog lifted during training is allowed; a pre-committed per-game luck stream is action-independent and can be conditioned on, while the realised dice cannot (which attacks happened depends on the action). What no source measures is the project's blocking question: whether any of these makes a value head rank *alternative turns at one position* rather than positions across games. The nearest published routes are indirect: DORA's value targets are the one-step equilibrium value rather than the outcome ([DORA](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2110.02924)), and SAVE distils search Q-values back into the Q network and shows that without that amortisation loss a learned Q gets *worse* with larger test-time search budgets because poor values are used for bootstrapping inside the tree ([SAVE](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1912.02807)). Both are inferences for the turn-ranking gap; every auxiliary-head multiplier comes from from-scratch Go and none from an imitation-seeded run.

## Atomic-action search collapses on multi-action turns; sampled whole turns worked

The project's negative result, Gumbel MCTS with 32 simulations over about 350 atomic actions producing no gain over the prior, is what the search literature predicts on three counts. Danihelka's counterexample shows that with Q-values (0, 0, 1) and prior (0.5, 0.3, 0.2), picking the best of the top-2 most-probable actions yields 0 against the prior's 0.2, and the thesis states that when simulations are fewer than actions, UCT and AlphaZero do not ensure a policy improvement; Gumbel's guarantee of improvement in expectation holds for any simulation count *under the premise that the visited actions' values are correctly evaluated*, and on 9x9 Go MuZero fails to learn from 16 or fewer simulations while Gumbel learns at 2 ([Danihelka thesis](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10167022/2/ivo_danihelka_thesis.pdf); [mctx](https://github.com/google-deepmind/mctx)). A one-to-two-action lookahead inside a ten-decision turn evaluates mid-turn positions with a value head trained on other positions, so the premise is weak exactly where it is needed. Grill's regularised-target analysis adds that at five simulations most of the gain comes from the target formulation, not the search ([Grill 2020](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2007.12509)). And the multi-action-turn studies show the depth problem by construction: in Hero Academy (5 action points per turn, about 60 legal actions per decision), vanilla action-level MCTS ran **258,488 iterations per turn with leaves averaging 4.86 plies deep**, evaluated 201 unique end-of-turn outcomes against 9,344 for whole-turn evolution, and won 0% to 4.5% against the other search variants; once turns had 10 or more actions, whole-turn evolution won 55% or more against every MCTS variant ([Hero Academy](https://sebastianrisi.com/wp-content/uploads/justesen_tciaig17.pdf)). microRTS's FullGame8x8 map had an average joint-action branching factor of 7,922 and a maximum of 623,700, and UCB1 sampling was unsuited to it ([Ontañón 2013](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/12681/12681-52-16198-1-2-20201228.pdf)).

Every multi-action success reduces the turn to a small candidate set first. Prismata's Hierarchical Portfolio Search decomposes a turn into four phases, each with scripted partial players (12 in the Master bot), searches the cross product with UCT, and reached **Tier 6, the top 25% of the human ladder**, on one CPU thread with 3-second searches; search time buys strength monotonically (alpha-beta at 3 s averaged 71.8% against UCT at 100 ms's 28.3%) ([Prismata HPS](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/12787/12787-52-16304-1-2-20201228.pdf)). Arimaa's SHARP quantifies what an expert-move ranker does to a 17,000-move turn: keep the top 1% and it still holds **81.0% of expert moves**, keep 20% and it holds 99.2%; tactical move generators produce about 3% of legal moves yet capture about 97% of the evaluation gain, and adding them gave 80 Elo at once ([SHARP](https://icosahedral.net/downloads/djwu2015arimaa_color.pdf)). Sampled MuZero searches over K actions sampled from the prior and on 19x19 Go **K = 50 of 362 already approaches the all-actions baseline** ([Sampled MuZero](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2104.06303)). The Diplomacy line is the closest thing to a controlled comparison from an imitation seed: A2C initialised from the imitation policy did not improve, replicated in the BRPI appendix, while Sampled Best Response (a few candidate joint orders sampled from the current policy, evaluated against sampled opponent orders) beat both ([BRPI](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2006.04635)); DORA samples 250 joint orders per player, keeps the 50 most likely, runs one-step regret matching with a value network at the leaves, and its double-oracle expansion of candidates was worth **1023 ± 13 against 927 ± 13 Elo** in the two-player variant ([DORA](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2110.02924)). piKL regularises that one-step equilibrium toward the imitation anchor, and at inference alone, with no training, the imitation policy's 7.9% ± 0.4% population score becomes **32.9% ± 0.7% at λ = 10⁻³**, while λ = 10⁻¹ keeps human move-prediction accuracy at 43% for 16.5% score, a trade-off curve of the same shape as the project's end_turn offset sweep ([piKL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.07544)). Diplodocus, the RL version, ranked first in a 200-game human tournament (27% ± 4%) where pure self-play DORA ranked 32nd (13% ± 3%) ([Diplodocus](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.05492)).

Two calibrating results bound what any of this can buy. Jones's Hex scaling study finds each extra 10x of training compute removes about 15x of test-time search, down to a floor of a single-node search, so a strong prior substitutes for search rather than being replaced by it ([Jones 2021](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2104.03113)); and AlphaGo's RL policy beat its SL seed in more than 80% of games yet the *SL* policy was the better MCTS prior, because humans "select a diverse beam of promising moves" while RL "optimizes for the single best move" ([AlphaGo](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/alphago/AlphaGoNaturePaper.pdf)). "Beats the seed head-to-head" and "is a better prior" are different measurements. ExIt, the one two-player expert-iteration result with a stated budget, used **10,000 MCTS iterations per move** on 9x9 Hex ([ExIt](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08439)); nothing in the survey supports 32-simulation atomic search as a teacher, and no published AlphaZero-style result exists for whole-turn search in a two-player perfect-information wargame.

The transfer for Wesnoth is a turn-level, KL-regularised, sampled search: draw K whole turns from the autoregressive seed, score each with the value head after sampling opponent replies (or, cheaper, at end of own turn), select by value plus λ·log prior with Gumbel or piKL rules, and measure by the standing 800-game match. It costs K forward passes per turn and no training. Its kill criterion is the project's own blocking finding: if the value head cannot rank K sampled turns better than the prior's argmax, the search is a random draw with extra latency, and the literature's only answer to that is the value-side section above. The portfolio variant is the RCA candidate actions themselves plus seed-sampled plans as partial players, the exact combat enumerator as leaf evaluator, CPU-only, with Prismata as its precedent; its known failure modes are choice-point design ("they must be potentially useful, otherwise they are just increasing the branching factor") ([Puppet Search](https://skatgame.net/mburo/ps/TCIAIG17-puppet.pdf)) and exploitability of script restriction (an alpha-beta best response to PGS's dominant script scored 0.290 against it at 8 units) ([PGS](https://skatgame.net/mburo/ps/combat13.pdf)).

## Credit-assignment decompositions and group baselines have no two-player evidence

The methods built for delayed rewards were validated on single-agent tasks only. RUDDER regresses the sequence return with an LSTM and redistributes it as the step-to-step difference of the prediction; on Atari at 200M frames it took Bowling from 56 to 192 and Solaris from 616 to 1,827, but the LSTM was "restricted to sequence chunks of 500 frames", the authors warn it "has problems with very long sequences" and that "reward redistribution may introduce disturbing spurious reward signals", and there is no two-player test ([RUDDER](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.07857)). Align-RUDDER exists for the 2-to-10-demonstration regime and assumes about 20 event symbols and one shared strategy; its one complex result is a diamond in 0.1% of Minecraft runs ([Align-RUDDER](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.14108)), so with 17,000 games and 21 maps it buys nothing. Hindsight Credit Assignment is tabular only, with scalability "an exciting problem for future research" ([HCA](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.02503)); CCA's experiments are a contextual bandit and Key-to-Door, where it solves in far fewer than the 3·10⁹ steps vanilla actor-critic needs but "suffers from a decrease in performance" at low exogenous noise because the statistic is not perfectly action-independent ([CCA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.09464)); TVT needs an external-memory architecture and warns of spurious splice events ([TVT](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.06721)); Synthetic Returns assumes a single future state where past contributions land ([Synthetic Returns](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.12425)). The credit-assignment survey excludes multi-agent settings entirely and names "the minimum rate of successes" a method needs as an open question ([Pignatelli survey](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.01072)). All of these re-time the one bit or lower the variance of its estimate; none adds information, and RUDDER's redistribution credits only the action taken along the realised trajectory, which is the within-state contrast the project already lacks.

Group baselines look attractive with a forkable simulator, and the arithmetic says where they pay. RLOO uses k = 2 or 4 samples per prompt with a leave-one-out baseline and beats PPO by 10 to 32 points on LLM reward models ([RLOO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14740)); GRPO samples 64 outputs per question and normalises by the group standard deviation ([GRPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03300)). Neither faces a binary reward near p = 0.5. There, the leave-one-out baseline from k samples has standard error 0.5/√(k−1), and detecting a per-decision effect of size δ needs on the order of p(1−p)/δ² paired samples per state, about **100 for δ = 0.05**, which is the project's measured mean best-turn gain; GRPO's division by the group standard deviation turns any mixed group into ±O(1) advantages regardless of the true effect, amplifying luck (a property of the formulas, not a reported finding). The many-actions theorem gives the decision rule: sampling N actions per state is unbiased and lower-variance than one, but beats a proportionally longer trajectory only when "action-dependent covariance constitutes a sizeable portion of the total" variance, and is "never optimal" for bandits when an extra action sample costs as much as an extra state sample ([Many-actions PG](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v202/nauman23a.html)). In a 300-decision game with combat dice, most variance at one atomic decision is downstream, so atomic-level groups are predicted to lose to more games; the form the theory favours is a per-turn contrast under common random numbers, truncated by the value head (the CRN-rollouts estimator for policies "identical beyond some time step"). GiGPO gets step-level groups for free by finding recurring states across trajectories (over 65% recur in ALFWorld) ([GiGPO](https://personal.ntu.edu.sg/boan/papers/NeurIPS25_GiGPO.pdf)); a wargame with hundreds of units will not recur naturally, and the forkable simulator can manufacture the recurrence only at vine's rollout cost. The turn-level critic idea has one number, ArCHer's "100x more sample efficient than ... PPO" on multi-turn language tasks, with a few turns and no adversary ([ArCHer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.19446)); it maps onto a critic trained across side-turn boundaries (30 to 60 per game instead of 300 decisions) and is untested against a learning opponent. Salimans and Chen's backward curriculum from a single demonstration, which a resettable simulator enables, reduces "exponential ... to quadratic scaling" in the blind-cliff-walk analysis, reached 74,500 on Montezuma's Revenge, and "much less well" on Gravitar and Pitfall; its multi-agent use is the authors' untested claim ([Salimans & Chen](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.03381)).

## Cheap self-play that beat a seed was search-free with a pool and an anchor; hierarchies did not pay

Two documented cases improved an imitation seed by self-play at a budget within an order of magnitude of this project's. AlphaGo's RL policy, initialised from the SL policy and trained by REINFORCE with a baseline against a pool of past checkpoints for one day on 50 GPUs, won more than 80% against its seed and 85% against Pachi with no search ([AlphaGo](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/alphago/AlphaGoNaturePaper.pdf)). Generals.io's 2025 preprint cloned 16,320 curated replays in 3 hours on one H100, ran 36 hours of PPO against an opponent pool of 3, and went from **1,874 to 2,052 Elo**, of which about 100 Elo is attributed to self-play and the rest to shaping and population play, landing in the top 25 of the human 1v1 ladder ([Generals.io](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.06825v1)). RAISocketAI's behaviour-cloning start then PPO fine-tune reads **71% (BC only) against 88% (BC then PPO)** with 100M-step fine-tunes at 1.7 to 5.3 A10 GPU-days each and 50% past-self opponents, and the paper credits imitation with providing "non-zero win-loss rewards" early where pure RL sees none ([RAISocketAI](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.08112v1)). The Lux winner kept a frozen teacher's policy in the loss as a KL term "to stabilize behavior and prevent strategic cycles" on a personal dual-GPU PC ([Toad Brigade](https://github.com/IsaiahPressman/Kaggle_Lux_AI_2021/blob/main/README.md)). Three warnings sit beside these: the Diplomacy A2C failure from an imitation seed in a multi-unit game; AlphaGo's improved policy being a worse search prior; and Gym-microRTS's self-play variants underperforming a fixed pool of scripted opponents at 300M steps ([Gym-microRTS](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2105.13807)), which does not transfer here because CoacAI was near the top of that field while Wesnoth's RCA AI is weak relative to the imitation seed. A recipe assembled from these pieces (checkpoint pool with PFSP weighting, KL anchor to the seed, TD(λ) value with UPGO, a value warm-up) is measured piecewise in Go, SC2, Generals and microRTS and nowhere as a whole, and the project's own legs, which never beat their prior, are the local prior on it.

Hierarchical and neuro-symbolic decompositions have the worst record at small budgets, and their positive results are not what they look like. Tencent's HMS won 75-25 against the same micro model without the macro layer, and JueWu-SL's full model beat its no-macro ablation 69-31, but both are supervised same-data ablations, not comparisons against an end-to-end agent of equal compute ([HMS](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.07887); [JueWu-SL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.12582)). Where the RL baseline was already strong, the macro layer added 97.81% against 97.77% win rate versus humans, at 830 V100s for 420 hours ([MGG](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2021/file/86dba86754c0ad93997a11fa947d97b2-Paper.pdf)). On the wargame side, Atlatl's full HRL "did not show improved performance", its feudal manager-worker hierarchy "failed to demonstrate any learning", and the one large gain came from a score-prediction network *selecting among eight fixed models per step*, **130.5 against the best single model's 80.3, a 62.6% improvement** ([Atlatl dissertation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.13333)). Nachum's analysis attributes most observed HRL benefit to exploration rather than easier learning ([Nachum 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.10618)); Pang's controller and sub-policies destabilised each other when updated together at high difficulty ([Pang 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.09095)); TStarBot1's 165-macro-action agent beat the cheating built-in AI but "will lose consistently once a human player figures out [its] preference for Zergling Rush" ([TStarBots](https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.07193)); and CherryPi's learned build-order switcher, the one high-level choice fit purely to outcomes (2.8M games with random switches), reached 0.878-0.888 against its training bots but 0.635 and 0.706 against held-out ones, and the authors report that gains of the memory-observation variants on the training bots "can not be transferred to held-out bots" ([Gehring 2018](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1811.08568)). The wargaming survey's summary is that script abstraction speeds learning "by orders of magnitude ... at a cost in loss of flexibility" ([Goodman 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.08922)). The one Wesnoth-native precedent fits this pattern exactly: a 2012 neural-network recruiter inside the fixed RCA structure beat the default recruiter **66.6% of 2,363 games and 69.3% of 1,179**, trained on about 600 games per two hours ([ML Recruiter](https://wiki.wesnoth.org/Machine_Learning_Recruiter)). A narrow learned component measured over thousands of cheap games was affordable and detectable in 2012; a learned hierarchy over the seed has no measured gain in any wargame.

## Conclusion

The survey inverts the usual framing of the project's problem. The assets it holds, a bit-exact forkable simulator with god view, an exact per-fight enumerator, and 17,000 rated human games with full trajectories, are precisely the inputs the *measured* levers consume, and not one of those levers requires search: a KL anchor, a privileged critic, a filtered fine-tune, an offline actor-critic pass, exact auxiliary heads and a terminal relabel are all changes to the loss, the label or the critic's input, each verifiable by a match that costs cents. The information budget explains why the order matters: a self-play leg yields about one bit per game, the corpus already holds about 17,000 such bits and ten million demonstrated decisions, and the only published way an RL leg out-teaches that is by generating games where the seed is wrong (exploiter branching on z, surprise weighting, a pool that punishes forgetting) while an anchor keeps it from unlearning what it knows.

The blocking question is one the literature does not answer. Every turn-level search option is conditional on a value head that ranks alternative turns at one position, the project has measured that its head does not, and no paper tests any method against that specific capability; the value-side levers are the only published route to it, and DORA's search-derived value targets and SAVE's amortisation loss are inferences, not measurements. The practical consequence is a gate rather than a recommendation: before any turn-level search or self-play leg is priced, measure directly whether a value head trained with the auxiliary heads and the god-view input ranks K seed-sampled turns better than the prior's argmax on the positions where turn-level gaps are known to exist. If it does, piKL-style decode is a free +Elo test and the search loop has a teacher; if it does not, the corpus-side options are the whole affordable frontier, and the 2012 recruiter result is the reminder that a narrow learned component inside a fixed structure is the one thing that has ever been shown to work in Wesnoth itself.
